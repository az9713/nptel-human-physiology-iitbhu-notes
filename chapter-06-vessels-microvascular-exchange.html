06. Blood vessels and microvascular exchange

During a long walk, working leg muscle needs more oxygen without waiting for a new vascular network to grow. After prolonged standing, a different question becomes visible: why can fluid accumulate around an ankle even though blood is still circulating? Increasing blood flow and preventing swelling are related transport problems, but they are not the same problem. A useful explanation must distinguish flow along a vessel, exchange across its wall, and removal of tissue fluid through lymphatic vessels.

The topic spine follows official NPTEL L15, Blood Vessels & Capillary. The complete caption text and a separately recorded bounded review of 71 sampled stills plus five targeted frames establish source coverage, not continuous audio/video review. The hydraulic derivation, synthetic regional balances and controlled models are original teaching constructions. Research extensions qualify classical exchange and organ-specific control; no trajectory is a patient measurement. Sources and evidence limits.

6.1 Vessel architecture sets the available routes L15 02:08

An artery carries blood away from the heart; a vein carries it toward the heart. These names specify direction within a circuit. They do not define oxygen content: pulmonary arteries carry blood from the right heart toward the lungs, and pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood to the left heart. A lumen is the interior space through which blood flows. The endothelium is the specialized cellular lining contacting that blood. The wall and lumen must be identified separately because changing the wall's contraction can change the lumen's hydraulic properties.

A systemic route can be organized as elastic artery, muscular artery, arteriole, capillary network, venule and vein. An elastic artery, such as the aorta, stores part of the ejection energy by stretching and releases it as its wall recoils. A muscular artery distributes blood toward an organ through a wall with substantial smooth muscle. Smooth muscle is contractile tissue that does not have the striated organization of skeletal muscle. In small arteries and arterioles, changes in its contraction alter resistance to flow. A venule collects blood downstream of capillaries. Larger veins have comparatively compliant walls: their volume can change appreciably with pressure. OpenStax §20.1 supplies the structural framework; Figure 6.1 adds separately sourced human histology.

The wall of a larger artery or vein has three organizational layers, or tunics. The intima includes endothelium and its supporting region. The media contains smooth muscle and varying elastic material. The externa, also called adventitia, is connective tissue outside the media. Their relative thickness varies with vessel class and location. A vein is not simply an artery recolored blue, and a capillary is not a small tube with the same three thick layers. Thin exchange walls and muscular pressure-regulating walls solve different tasks.

Figure 6.2 changes specimen, scale and preparation. Its mouse capillaries provide lumen-facing ultrastructural examples; they are not smaller versions of the human artery–vein fields, and they do not supply fenestrated or sinusoidal examples.

A capillary is a selective exchange boundary L15 04:06

A capillary is a microscopic blood vessel specialized for exchange between blood and tissue. Its endothelial cells rest on a supporting extracellular layer, the basal lamina or basement membrane. This is not an outer layer of epithelial cells. Pericytes are mural cells associated with many microvessels, sharing close relationships with endothelium and its basement membrane. They participate in support, signaling and context-dependent contractile functions. Their morphology and contractile phenotype vary along the vascular tree; calling every pericyte a smooth-muscle cell erases an important distinction. The NCBI capillary histology account provides the introductory cell and barrier categories; these definitions do not treat every mural cell as an identical sphincter.

Continuous capillaries lack endothelial fenestrations, but “continuous” does not mean impermeable. A solute may cross through a suitable cellular route or through regulated intercellular pathways. Tissue-specific junctions matter: the brain's endothelial barrier is more restrictive than a generic muscle capillary. Fenestrated capillaries have pores through endothelial cells, called fenestrae; these are not gaps large enough for ordinary red cells to pass through freely. Associated diaphragms and basement membranes help determine selectivity, and kidney glomerular filtration adds specialized structures beyond the endothelial pore alone.

Discontinuous or sinusoidal blood capillaries have a more open, tissue-specific organization, including discontinuities in endothelial and supporting structures. Liver, spleen and bone marrow illustrate specialized blood sinusoidal systems. Bone-marrow cell entry into blood cannot be generalized to every capillary pore. A lymph node's lymphatic sinus carries lymph within a lymphatic compartment; it is not a blood sinusoid merely because both structures use the word sinus. These distinctions repair the source's compressed category comparison without turning its three labels into a universal molecular-sieving table.

Capillarity and circulation have different driving forces

Capillarity is an interfacial phenomenon familiar from liquid rise in a narrow glass tube. Surface tension, contact angle, tube geometry and gravity determine the static rise or depression. Adhesion describes interactions between different materials; cohesion describes interactions within the liquid. The water–glass example usefully introduces small spaces, but it does not provide the continuous energy source that circulates blood through a filled vascular network. Interfacial capillary rise involves a liquid interface; the pressure-driven circulation model below concerns blood occupying a connected lumen.

The relevant distinction is energetic. A static raised column has a finite gravitational energy relative to its starting state. Sustained viscous flow dissipates energy continuously and therefore requires continued mechanical work, supplied principally by the heart and modified by vascular elastic storage and other mechanisms. No inference that small vessels run indefinitely by capillary attraction follows from their name. The lecture's capillarity analogy is retained as a distinction to examine, rather than treated as an experimentally established blood pump.

Derive resistance for a specified tube L15 06:01

For an original teaching model, let a straight circular tube have radius \(r>0\), length \(L>0\), and a constant dynamic viscosity \(\mu>0\), in meters, meters and pascal-seconds. Define pressure drop \(\Delta P=P_{\mathrm{in}}-P_{\mathrm{out}}\), in pascals, and volumetric flow rate \(Q_{\mathrm{flow}}\), in cubic meters per second, positive from inlet to outlet. Assume a rigid wall, no slip at the wall, an incompressible Newtonian fluid, steady laminar flow, and a fully developed axial velocity profile. A Newtonian fluid has shear stress proportional to the velocity gradient with constant \(\mu\). These assumptions are the price of an exact ideal-tube formula.

Let \(s\) be distance from the tube axis, with \(0\leq s\leq r\), and \(u(s)\) the axial velocity. Balance the pressure force on a cylindrical fluid core of radius \(s\) against viscous traction on its lateral surface. The pressure force is \(\Delta P\,\pi s^2\), while traction magnitude is \(\tau(s)2\pi sL\); hence \(\tau(s)=\Delta P\,s/(2L)\). For positive flow the speed decreases outward, so the Newtonian relation is \(-\mu\,du/ds=\tau(s)\). Integrate this gradient using the no-slip condition \(u(r)=0\):

\[u(s)=\frac{\Delta P}{4\mu L}(r^2-s^2),\qquad Q_{\mathrm{flow}}=\int_0^r u(s)\,2\pi s\,ds=\frac{\pi r^4\Delta P}{8\mu L}.\tag{6.1}\]

The annular area element \(2\pi s\,ds\) is essential: averaging the centerline speed over the radius without area weighting would give the wrong total. The integration produces two geometric factors of radius from area and two from the velocity profile. Define hydraulic resistance as \(R_{\mathrm{flow}}=\Delta P/Q_{\mathrm{flow}}\), using the linear relation's limiting ratio at zero pressure drop. Then

\[R_{\mathrm{flow}}=\frac{8\mu L}{\pi r^4},\qquad \overline u=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{flow}}}{\pi r^2}=\frac{\Delta P r^2}{8\mu L}.\tag{6.2}\]

Resistance has units Pa·s/m³. In (6.1), \(r^4\Delta P/(\mu L)\) has units m³/s, so the expression is dimensionally a flow. Zero pressure drop gives zero net flow; reversing its sign reverses flow within this idealization. Doubling length or viscosity halves flow at fixed radius and pressure drop. Real blood is a cellular suspension, vessels deform, and pressure is pulsatile. In capillaries, cell size and cell–wall interactions particularly limit a constant-viscosity continuum treatment. The fourth-power relation is a conditional sensitivity law, not a claim that one can infer a patient's flow from a radius photograph alone.

The chapter program evaluates a synthetic tube with \(\Delta P=1000\) Pa, \(r=20\) μm, \(L=1\) mm and \(\mu=0.003\) Pa·s. Using 1 μm = \(10^{-6}\) m and 1 mm = \(10^{-3}\) m gives \(Q_{\mathrm{flow}}=2.094395102\times10^{-11}\) m³/s, or 1.256637 μL/min. The microliter conversion uses \(10^9\) μL/m³ and 60 s/min. These inputs are selected for calculation, not measured arteriolar parameters.

If radius falls to 80% of its initial value while the other inputs stay fixed, flow becomes \(0.8^4=0.4096\) of baseline and resistance becomes 2.441406 times baseline. If radius rises by 20%, flow becomes 2.0736 times baseline. This asymmetry matters for the walking muscle: modest smooth-muscle relaxation can strongly alter a segment's resistance. It does not follow that total muscle flow doubles, because pressures and the other series/parallel resistances can change too.

The network changes the meaning of “narrow” L15 08:03

Write \(Q\) for the already defined volumetric flow \(Q_{\mathrm{flow}}\), and \(R\) for hydraulic resistance \(R_{\mathrm{flow}}\), in the network formulas. For steady incompressible flow through one unbranched vessel with negligible wall exchange, conservation requires the same volume per second at each cross-section. If cross-sectional area is \(A\) and mean velocity is \(\overline u\), then \(Q_{\mathrm{flow}}=A\overline u\). At a fixed flow, reducing area raises mean velocity. The fixed-flow condition is different from (6.1), where the pressure drop was fixed and flow changed with radius. Holding both pressure drop and flow fixed while independently changing radius would overconstrain that tube model.

A capillary bed is a set of many branching exchange paths. At a junction with no storage, the sum of incoming flows equals the sum of outgoing flows. In a simple bed of \(N\) identical parallel capillaries, each of area \(A_c\), a total bed flow \(Q_b\) divides into \(Q_b/N\) per capillary. The mean speed is \(Q_b/(N A_c)\). A small individual lumen can therefore coexist with a large total cross-sectional area and slow mean transit through the bed. Comparing one aortic cross-section with one capillary cross-section misses the parallel multiplicity.

For linear hydraulic resistances in series, the same flow passes each segment and pressure drops add. If segment resistances are \(R_1,R_2,\ldots\), the equivalent resistance is \(R_{\mathrm{series}}=\sum_jR_j\). In parallel, every branch has the same inlet-to-outlet pressure difference and branch flows add, so \(1/R_{\mathrm{parallel}}=\sum_j1/R_j\). Both results follow directly by inserting \(Q=\Delta P/R\) into conservation; they do not require all branches to have the same radius. Two equal resistances of 2 arbitrary consistent units give 4 in series and 1 in parallel. Losing one of the parallel paths doubles the remaining pair's equivalent resistance from 1 to 2.

The words metarteriole, thoroughfare channel and precapillary sphincter label a familiar microcirculatory teaching architecture: a connecting route, its downstream channel, and localized contractile control near capillary entry. They are useful when that architecture is actually present. They are not established universal components of every human tissue. Sakai and Hosoyamada's historical review traces the tissue-specific observations behind the generalized diagram. Upstream resistance vessels, heterogeneous mural cells and endothelial signals must remain distinct from an imaginary ring at every capillary mouth.

For our walking-muscle case, the hydraulic question is now well posed: which resistance changes, which pressure difference drives the bed, and how many routes receive flow? More perfused paths may change effective exchange area as well as conductance. None of these statements yet predicts ankle fluid accumulation. That requires a separate balance across the endothelial wall.

6.2 Wall exchange and lymphatic return determine tissue fluid L15 12:11

Interstitial fluid is the extracellular fluid between tissue cells; it is outside the vascular lumen. Filtration here means net water movement from blood across the microvascular wall into that interstitium. Reabsorption means net movement in the opposite direction. Neither word means blood moving from arteriole to venule along the lumen. A vessel can simultaneously carry blood downstream and filter a much smaller fluid stream outward.

The hydrostatic pressure of a fluid is its mechanical pressure, expressed as force per area. Gravity contributes to differences with height, but pressure need not vanish when gravity is absent. The oncotic, or colloid osmotic, pressure is the osmotic contribution associated with macromolecules such as plasma proteins across a specified selective barrier. Albumin is a major plasma protein in this context. Hemoglobin is normally inside erythrocytes; it should not be substituted for freely dissolved plasma albumin in an intact-capillary osmotic model.

Small solutes and proteins must be distinguished because barrier selectivity determines the sustained osmotic effect. A high total dissolved-particle concentration on both sides does not by itself identify the effective opposing pressure for filtration. As in Chapter 2's compartment model, the route and the substance are part of the definition. Pressure-driven bulk water transfer and diffusion of an oxygen molecule can occur together, without being interchangeable transport mechanisms.

Classical Starling accounting: derive the signs L15 14:08

For a classical lumped model, define \(P_c\) and \(P_i\) as capillary and interstitial hydrostatic pressures, and \(\pi_c\) and \(\pi_i\) as plasma and bulk interstitial oncotic pressures. All four use mmHg, a physiological pressure unit; calculations must keep them in the same unit. Let \(L_p>0\) be hydraulic conductivity of a selected barrier, in volume/(area·time·pressure), and \(A_e\) its exchange area. Their product \(K_f=L_pA_e\) is a filtration coefficient in volume/(time·pressure). Let \(\sigma\) be a dimensionless protein reflection coefficient between zero and one: one represents an ideal fully effective osmotic difference for that protein, while zero removes its effective oncotic opposition in this model.

With outward fluid transfer \(J_v\) positive, the mechanical pressure difference favors outward movement when \(P_c>P_i\). A larger plasma oncotic pressure favors inward movement across a protein-selective barrier. Combining the opposing terms defines the classical net driving pressure \(D_{\mathrm{cl}}\):

\[D_{\mathrm{cl}}=(P_c-P_i)-\sigma(\pi_c-\pi_i),\qquad J_v=K_fD_{\mathrm{cl}}.\tag{6.3}\]

The equation is a constitutive model, not a derivation of endothelial biology from force balance alone. It separates driving pressure from conductance. If the bracket is positive but \(K_f\) tends to zero, filtration tends to zero. If \(\sigma=0\), the specified protein difference contributes no term. Calling 11 mmHg a “flow” would lose that distinction: pressure must be multiplied by an appropriate coefficient to obtain volume per time.

Use the source's classical numerical construction, with \(\sigma=1\), \(P_i=0\), \(\pi_c=25\) and \(\pi_i=1\) mmHg. At an arterial-end capillary pressure \(P_c=35\) mmHg, (6.3) gives \(35-0-(25-1)=+11\) mmHg. At \(P_c=17\) mmHg it gives \(17-0-(25-1)=-7\) mmHg. The signs designate outward filtration and inward reabsorption in this fixed-pressure model. The source arithmetic is consistent; its limitation is the physiological generality of the fixed-compartment picture.

Even this cartoon does not imply equal total outward and inward transfer. As a teaching extension, let pressure decline linearly along an otherwise uniform exchange length from 35 to 17 mmHg. Its mean is 26 mmHg, so the mean classical driving pressure is \(26-24=2\) mmHg. If the entire segment's synthetic \(K_f=0.01\) mL/(min·mmHg), the net outward transfer is 0.020 mL/min. An endpoint sign is insufficient to calculate a total without exchange-area weighting and a spatial pressure profile. The example also provides no universal fraction returned through capillaries versus lymph.

The relevant protein compartment can be microscopic

The endothelial glycocalyx is a macromolecular surface layer associated with the luminal endothelial surface. The subglycocalyx region is the small space immediately beyond that selective layer along a water pathway. Its protein environment need not equal well-mixed bulk interstitial fluid. This separation is the key research extension: changing bulk tissue protein concentration does not automatically impose the same immediate protein concentration at the effective filtering interface.

In experiments on cannulated nonfenestrated rat mesenteric microvessels, Adamson and colleagues (2004) combined controlled fluid-flux measurements with analysis of albumin distribution and pathway geometry. The results supported an oncotic difference associated with the glycocalyx and a protected protein environment. The preparation was a specific animal microvascular system, not a measurement of every human capillary. Its scientific role here is to challenge identification of bulk interstitial fluid with the directly opposing protein compartment.

Let \(\pi_g\) denote subglycocalyx oncotic pressure, in mmHg. A commonly used revised expression replaces \(\pi_i\) in the oncotic term:

\[J_v=L_pA_e\left[(P_c-P_i)-\sigma(\pi_c-\pi_g)\right].\tag{6.4}\]

This expression names a different interface; it does not by itself determine \(\pi_g\). Filtration can carry protein away from the protected region while diffusion and protein transport repopulate it. Consequently \(\pi_g\), pressure and fluid transfer can be coupled. Substituting one arbitrary fixed \(\pi_g\) into (6.4) and drawing a time curve would not establish real human filtration kinetics. A kinetic prediction requires additional transport relations, geometry, initial conditions and parameter evidence.

For many tissues in a maintained state, the revised account supports net filtration with lymphatic return rather than routine sustained arterial-end filtration followed by venous-end reabsorption. A sudden pressure reduction can produce transient reabsorption while local protein conditions adjust. Specialized absorbing microvascular beds require their own boundary conditions. Thus “no capillary ever absorbs” is as misleading as a universal two-end absorption cartoon. Woodcock and Michel (2021) review these distinctions; their veterinary/physiological review is used for mechanism, without importing fluid-therapy prescriptions.

Figure 6.4 separates the calculable classical endpoint construction from the revised interface question. Its revised phases identify compartments rather than assigning an unsupported fluid time course.

Swelling is a mismatch between entry and removal L15 18:10

Edema is excess fluid accumulation in the interstitial tissue compartment. It is an outcome with multiple possible causes. Reduced plasma albumin can reduce effective oncotic opposition; raised microvascular hydrostatic pressure can increase the outward mechanical drive; altered barrier conductivity or selectivity can change transfer; impaired lymphatic transport can reduce removal. These mechanisms can coexist. The presence of swelling alone does not identify which changed.

In protein-losing kidney disease, loss of albumin into urine can lower its plasma concentration; that loss does not mean the same albumin has been deposited into ankle interstitial fluid. Barrier damage causing protein escape into a local tissue is a different route. Sodium and water retention and changes in blood volume can add further effects. The lecture's albumin/edema example is therefore separated into protein loss, local leak and fluid balance, rather than treating urine and interstitium as one receiving compartment.

Let \(V_I(t)\) be fluid volume in a specified tissue region, in mL, and let \(Q_L(t)\geq0\) be lymphatic outflow, in mL/min. If \(J_v\) denotes the region's net vascular input in the same units and no other routes are included, conservation gives

\[\frac{dV_I}{dt}=J_v-Q_L,\qquad \Delta V_I=(J_v-Q_L)T\quad\text{for constant rates over }T.\tag{6.5}\]

The elapsed interval \(T\) uses minutes in the second expression. Zero storage change can coexist with nonzero filtration and drainage. A synthetic region receiving 0.30 mL/min and draining 0.30 mL/min accumulates zero over 20 min. If drainage falls to 0.10 mL/min at the same input, it accumulates 4 mL. Raising input to 0.50 while drainage stays 0.30 produces the same 4 mL. The program tracks blood-region loss and a separate lymph-return compartment, so every transferred milliliter is accounted for.

These prescribed rates are a short-interval accounting experiment. Real tissue pressure rises as storage changes, potentially opposing further filtration and modifying lymph formation. Lymphatic pumping and outlet conditions can also change. A sustained fixed-rate forecast would require evidence that those feedback effects remain negligible. The walking/standing case now has a specific ambiguity: increased ankle volume constrains the difference between entry and removal, while the two rates require independent information.

Alternative paths help only if they can carry enough flow L15 22:01

An anastomosis is a vascular connection between routes. Arterial–arterial and venous–venous connections can provide alternative paths; an arteriovenous connection connects arterial and venous sides without the usual intervening exchange route. A normal capillary network connecting an arteriole to a venule should not therefore be equated with every direct arteriovenous shunt. A collateral route can carry blood around a restriction, subject to its conductance and driving pressure.

The circle of Willis is an arterial network at the base of the brain, not a ring of ordinary capillaries. Its potential for redistribution does not guarantee sufficient perfusion after any obstruction: anatomical variation, pressure gradients and resistance of available routes matter. The parallel-resistance derivation already explains the limitation. A path can be connected topologically while carrying too little flow to meet tissue demand. In a venous obstruction, raised upstream venous pressure may promote edema; tissue ischemia, insufficient perfusion for its needs, and hemorrhage, blood outside a ruptured vessel, are distinct outcomes rather than synonyms for that pressure rise.

6.3 Local control changes resistance within limits L15 24:08

Vasodilation is an increase in vessel lumen caliber; vasoconstriction is a decrease. In resistance vessels, relaxation or contraction of smooth muscle contributes to these changes. An increase in tissue metabolism can be followed by increased local flow, called active hyperemia. This is a physiological response to demand. It differs from autoregulation, the tendency to stabilize a tissue's flow despite changes in its perfusion pressure over a bounded operating range.

During walking, working muscle changes its local chemical environment through oxygen consumption and production and removal of metabolites. Carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions and other signals can influence local vascular tone; endothelial and conducted signals help coordinate responses across vessels. A metabolic response links local chemical conditions to resistance. A myogenic response is a response of vascular smooth muscle to changes in mechanical stretch or pressure. These are empirical biological mechanisms. The algebra \(Q=\Delta P/R\) explains their possible hydraulic consequences but cannot prove which signaling mechanism caused a measured dilation.

The source's carbon dioxide/proton example is useful as a local-control motif, but it should not imply that lactic acid simply paralyzes every vascular muscle. The effect depends on the tissue, signal combination and state. Neural and endocrine signals act alongside local mechanisms. In working muscle, local dilation and sympathetic regulation can interact; there is no single “all sympathetic” or “all local” switch for the entire capillary bed. OpenStax §20.4 distinguishes local, neural and hormonal regulation and the changing allocation of systemic flow.

Flow stabilization is a conditional feedback problem L15 26:03

Perfusion pressure is the pressure difference driving flow through a specified tissue. Mean arterial pressure is the arterial pressure averaged over a cardiac cycle; it is not itself every organ's inlet-to-outlet difference. Downstream pressure and external tissue pressure can matter, especially in an enclosed organ such as the brain. Nor is autoregulation a command that capillaries themselves uniformly squeeze. Resistance changes occur in relevant upstream and microvascular segments, with tissue-specific cellular participation.

If a tissue's pressure difference rises while resistance remains momentarily fixed, the simple hydraulic relation predicts an initial rise in flow. A subsequent resistance increase can return flow toward its previous value. At a lower pressure difference, reduced resistance can help preserve flow. The phrase “constriction reduces flow” needs a reference state: constriction can reduce the pressure-induced excess while leaving the final flow near its original level. It need not reduce flow below that level. Conversely, dilation cannot preserve flow when the available pressure becomes too small or the vessel has reached its dilation limit.

Define dimensionless normalized pressure \(p=\Delta P/\Delta P_0\), resistance \(\rho=R/R_0\), and flow \(q=Q/Q_0\), where zero-subscript quantities are positive baseline values obeying \(Q_0=\Delta P_0/R_0\). Thus \(q=p/\rho\). This paragraph introduces \(\rho\) as a resistance ratio, not fluid density. For an explicitly synthetic feedback demonstration, choose a target resistance ratio \(\rho_*=\min(1.5,\max(0.7,p))\), and let resistance approach it with an arbitrary time constant \(\tau=3\) s:

\[\tau\frac{d\rho}{dt}=\rho_*-\rho,\qquad q(t)=\frac{p(t)}{\rho(t)}.\tag{6.6}\]

The function \(\max\) selects the larger argument and \(\min\) the smaller, thereby bounding resistance between 0.7 and 1.5 baseline units at its target. This is an illustrative control closure, not a fitted cerebral or muscle response. The first-order equation says that a remaining resistance error shrinks exponentially under a constant target. Starting with \(\rho=1\), a pressure step from 1 to 1.3 at \(t=2\) s gives \(\rho(t)=1.3-0.3e^{-(t-2)/3}\) after the step. Immediately flow is 1.3 baseline units. At 5 s, resistance is 1.189636 and flow is 1.092771. At 11 s, resistance is 1.285064 and flow is 1.011623. Each value is computed by the chapter program.

The time unit belongs only to this synthetic model; it does not assign a measured response time to the brain. At a maintained pressure ratio 2, the resistance target saturates at 1.5 and final flow is \(2/1.5=1.333333\). A stabilizing mechanism therefore need not guarantee perfect stabilization outside its available range. The same exercise can represent a changed flow target during active hyperemia, but doing so would require a distinct demand signal. Pressure autoregulation and demand-driven flow increase solve different control tasks.

In Figure 6.5 the pressure trace stays elevated while resistance adapts; the simultaneous flow trace exposes both recovery and saturation.

Low oxygen has an organ-specific meaning L15 28:51

An alveolus is a lung air space participating in gas exchange. Ventilation supplies fresh gas to that space; perfusion supplies blood to its associated exchange network. Low tissue oxygen in many systemic beds can favor local vasodilation, increasing supply. In the lung, low alveolar oxygen can instead produce hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction in small pulmonary arteries. This tends to divert perfusion from poorly ventilated regions toward better ventilated regions.

The compartment being sensed is crucial. Saying “low oxygen always dilates vessels” would direct more blood toward a poorly ventilated lung region and could worsen matching. Sommer and colleagues' review places oxygen sensing and effector machinery in precapillary pulmonary arterial smooth muscle, with endothelial modulation. The response can improve regional ventilation–perfusion matching without repairing the damaged alveolar tissue. If alveolar hypoxia is widespread, constriction can raise pulmonary vascular resistance rather than find a large well-ventilated destination. The source lung-injury example therefore illustrates redistribution, not a guarantee that infection or tumor leaves oxygenation normal.

Exercise redistribution also has competing objectives L15 31:59

Sympathetic activity can constrict vessels in gastrointestinal and cutaneous circulations under appropriate conditions. The gastrointestinal tract is the digestive system; the cutaneous circulation supplies skin. Neither is physiologically unimportant. Their perfusion changes must be interpreted alongside digestive activity, thermoregulation, exercise intensity and the need to maintain arterial pressure. During sustained exercise, increased skin flow can be needed to transfer heat toward the environment. Thus a universal claim that exercise always lowers skin flow would conflict with this thermoregulatory requirement.

For the walker, flow allocation and swelling can consequently move in different directions. Working-muscle flow may rise through lower resistance and increased cardiac output, while skin flow depends partly on heat balance and ankle filtration depends partly on local hydrostatic conditions and drainage. A single “more circulation” scale cannot represent all three outcomes. A useful measurement plan records which bed, what pressure difference, what flow and what tissue-fluid storage are being compared.

A shunt, a refill sign and a vascular lesion are different observations L15 33:13

An arteriovenous malformation (AVM) is an abnormal vascular arrangement that bypasses the usual capillary exchange route through direct arterial–venous connections. Low-resistance shunting can alter local flow distribution and venous loading. It is not equivalent to a beneficial collateral arterial route. The NINDS AVM account distinguishes altered perfusion, bleeding and compression as possible effects. Its discussion includes several management approaches; the lecture's brief mention of embolization is not a universal treatment recommendation. Embolization here means a medically performed procedure intended to occlude selected vascular routes, with appropriateness depending on the specific lesion and clinical evidence.

Blanching is visible paling when pressure displaces blood from superficial vessels. Capillary refill time is the observed interval for color to return after release of a standardized compression. It is an indirect, context-sensitive observation, not a direct measurement of capillary pressure, endothelial permeability or whole-organ oxygen delivery. Site, temperature, applied pressure/duration and observation method affect interpretation. A systematic review of refill measurement in children documents such method effects in that population; its numeric pediatric thresholds are not transferred here into an adult self-diagnosis rule.

A port-wine stain is a type of cutaneous capillary malformation, not a label for every birthmark and not simply transient exercise dilation. The National Library of Medicine classification separates it from a capillary hemangioma. Macular degeneration affects the macula, the central retinal region needed for detailed vision. The National Eye Institute distinguishes dry and wet age-related forms; abnormal vessel growth is particularly associated with the neovascular, or wet, form. These examples motivate vascular mechanisms without collapsing distinct diseases into “bad capillaries.”

6.5 Six problems with worked solutions

Problem 6.1. Recover the fourth power

Derivation and assumption audit. Starting from cylindrical force balance, derive the velocity profile and total flow for a rigid tube. Explain which step uses the no-slip condition. At fixed pressure drop, what happens to ideal resistance and flow when radius falls by 20%? Identify two reasons the result may fail quantitatively in a real capillary.

Worked solution

A fluid core of radius \(s\) has axial pressure force \(\Delta P\pi s^2\) and opposing viscous force \(2\pi sL\tau\). Equating them gives \(\tau=\Delta Ps/(2L)\). With \(-\mu du/ds=\tau\), integration gives \(u=C-\Delta Ps^2/(4\mu L)\). No slip sets \(u(r)=0\), so \(C=\Delta Pr^2/(4\mu L)\). Integrating \(u\) over annuli gives \(Q=2\pi\Delta P(4\mu L)^{-1}\int_0^r(r^2s-s^3)ds=\pi\Delta Pr^4/(8\mu L)\).

At radius ratio 0.8, flow ratio is \(0.8^4=0.4096\), and resistance ratio is its reciprocal, 2.441406. The decrease in flow is 59.04% relative to baseline. This calculation keeps pressure, length and viscosity fixed. In a real capillary, deforming red cells and size-dependent apparent viscosity violate the uniform Newtonian continuum idealization; wall deformation and unsteady driving can violate the geometric and steady-flow assumptions. Listing those assumptions is more informative than calling the fourth power universally exact.

Problem 6.2. Resolve the velocity paradox

Counterexample and networks. A report argues that blood must travel fastest in capillaries because each has the smallest cross-section. Compare one tube that narrows at fixed flow with a bed of 100 equal parallel tubes, each having one tenth of an upstream tube's area. If the same total flow enters the bed, compare the mean speeds. What happens to equivalent resistance if one of two equal parallel paths is lost?

Worked solution

For one tube, conservation gives \(\overline u=Q/A\); smaller area at the same flow means greater speed. The bed instead has total area \(100(A_0/10)=10A_0\), where \(A_0\) is upstream area. Each capillary receives one hundredth of total flow. Its speed is \((Q/100)/(A_0/10)=Q/(10A_0)\), one tenth the upstream speed. The incorrect report used one branch's area while assigning it the entire bed's flow.

For two equal branches of resistance \(R\), total conductance is \(2/R\), so equivalent resistance is \(R/2\). Removing one branch leaves \(R\), twice the original value. At fixed inlet/outlet pressure, total flow halves. At fixed total flow, the required pressure drop doubles. These are different boundary conditions; the calculation must state which is imposed.

Problem 6.3. Audit a classical exchange cartoon

Calculation and interpretation. Use \(P_i=0\), \(\pi_c=25\), \(\pi_i=1\) mmHg and \(\sigma=1\). Compute driving pressures at \(P_c=35\) and 17 mmHg. If \(P_c\) falls linearly over a uniformly exchanging segment, find its mean driving pressure and net transfer with total \(K_f=0.01\) mL/(min·mmHg). Can these data establish a universal percentage of fluid reabsorbed?

Worked solution

The oncotic difference is 24 mmHg. The endpoint drives are \(35-24=11\) and \(17-24=-7\) mmHg. Positive denotes filtration and negative reabsorption under the classical constitutive law. The source arithmetic requires no correction. With linear pressure decline and uniform exchange coefficient per length, the spatial mean pressure is \((35+17)/2=26\), giving mean drive 2 mmHg and net transfer \(0.01\times2=0.020\) mL/min outward.

The result is net outward transfer despite an inward endpoint. It uses a specified spatial profile and weighting; the endpoints alone would not determine that integral. It cannot establish a universal reabsorption fraction because neither all tissue architectures nor their protein/interface conditions have been specified. Even a calculated classical fraction for this segment would remain conditional, and the revised account requires distinguishing subglycocalyx from bulk interstitial protein.

Problem 6.4. Design a swelling-discrimination experiment

Conservation and inference. Two synthetic tissue regions both gain 4 mL over 20 min. Region A has filtration 0.30 and lymph flow 0.10 mL/min; region B has filtration 0.50 and lymph flow 0.30 mL/min. Starting from (100, 20, 0) mL in blood, interstitium and return, find final compartment volumes. Explain why low plasma albumin, local protein leak and lymph obstruction should not be assigned the same parameter change.

Worked solution

For A, 6 mL leaves the blood region, 2 mL reaches the return compartment, and the difference of 4 mL remains in tissue. Final volumes are (94, 24, 2) mL. For B, transfers are 10 and 6 mL, yielding (90, 24, 6). Both conserve 120 mL. Tissue volume alone therefore cannot distinguish the two mechanisms; an independent measurement of vascular input or drainage, or a verified selective perturbation of one route, is required.

Low plasma albumin changes the luminal protein environment; a local leak may change conductivity, protein selectivity and local protein concentrations; lymph obstruction primarily changes the removal route and its pressure–flow behavior. Each can produce secondary changes in the others. The simple constant-rate experiment isolates entry/removal accounting but cannot turn these mechanisms into one interchangeable “edema coefficient.” Urinary albumin loss especially must not be counted automatically as ankle protein entry.

Problem 6.5. Find the controller's operating boundary

Computation and model diagnosis. In (6.6), pressure rises from 1 to 1.3 at 2 s, resistance initially equals 1, and \(\tau=3\) s. Derive resistance and flow after the step and evaluate them at 5 s. Then determine the long-time flow after a maintained step to 2. Does recovery prove that the model describes cerebral autoregulation?

Worked solution

At pressure 1.3 the target is 1.3. Separation of \(d(\rho-1.3)/dt=-(\rho-1.3)/3\) gives \(\rho=1.3-0.3e^{-(t-2)/3}\). At 5 s the exponential is \(e^{-1}\), so resistance is 1.189636 and flow \(1.3/1.189636=1.092771\). Flow initially jumped to 1.3 and then declined toward 1; resistance increased while the imposed pressure stayed high.

At pressure 2 the bounded target is 1.5, so long-time flow is \(2/1.5=1.333333\). Saturation prevents full recovery. Neither response establishes cerebral physiology: the target range, equation and time constant were selected, and real perfusion depends on more than normalized inlet pressure. The calculation demonstrates a possible feedback structure and a failure mode. Empirical identification would require measured responses, relevant outlet/external pressures and a test separating pressure control from metabolic demand.

Problem 6.6. Repair a universal vascular rule

Mechanism comparison and evidence judgment. A proposed rule says: “Low oxygen dilates every vessel; exercise constricts all skin vessels; connected collateral arteries prevent ischemia; a slow refill time proves a capillary leak.” Repair each statement. Which observation would distinguish a direct AV shunt from an alternative arterial supply route?

Worked solution

Low oxygen in many systemic tissue beds can favor dilation, while low alveolar oxygen can constrict precapillary pulmonary arteries, redistributing flow relative to ventilation. The oxygen compartment and organ must be stated. Exercise can increase sympathetic constrictor influences in some beds, but skin perfusion also supports heat removal and may rise during sustained exercise. A universal cutaneous decrease misses that competing task.

A collateral arterial connection provides a possible route, not guaranteed adequate flow. Its conductance and available pressure must meet the tissue's needs. Refill is an indirect color-return observation affected by measurement conditions; it does not directly measure hydraulic conductivity or protein escape. A leak claim needs evidence of transvascular transfer or barrier change, interpreted with pressure and drainage.

Anatomical/flow evidence that arterial blood enters a venous route without the normal intervening exchange network supports an AV shunt. A collateral arterial route feeds the downstream arterial/exchange supply by another path. Connectivity alone cannot distinguish them unless the connected vessel types, direction and destination are identified. A shunt can increase total measured flow while reducing effective tissue exchange; more flow in a named conduit does not by itself establish better oxygen delivery.
