05. The heart as an electrical and mechanical pump

A monitor shows a regular electrical rhythm while the circulation delivers too little blood. That combination is possible because an electrical recording does not measure how much blood leaves the ventricle. Successful delivery requires an excitation sequence, functioning muscle, a fillable chamber, pressure-appropriate valve opening and a connected vascular route. This chapter follows one beat from cellular excitation to regional flow, then constructs a synthetic cycle in which every changing chamber volume is accounted for by inflow or outflow. The model is deliberately simple enough that an inconsistent animated valve can be detected by an equation.

The topic spine is official NPTEL L13–L14. The caption texts and an existing bounded audit of seven contact sheets and seven targeted stills establish source sequence and selected slide details. Continuous video/audio was not reviewed. Anatomy and electrophysiology are checked against the cited sources; the pressure–volume cycle, circuit values, ECG timing trace and diagrams are original teaching constructions, not measured patient data. Source scope and provenance.

5.1 Chambers, valves and connected circuits L13 02:00

The heart is a muscular organ in the mediastinum, the central thoracic region between the lungs. Its apex points generally toward the person's left and its base includes the connections to major vessels. Anatomical right and left belong to the person; in a conventional anterior drawing, the right heart appears toward the viewer's left. A fist is a rough size comparison, not a measurement rule that excludes variation with body size, age or disease. Location “between chest and heart” in the caption is not a meaningful anatomical relationship.

The heart wall comprises an inner endocardium, a muscular myocardium and an outer epicardium. The epicardial surface is associated with the visceral layer of serous pericardium. The pericardium is the surrounding sac with fibrous and serous components; a lubricated potential space separates its opposed serous surfaces. Wall layers and surrounding sac are therefore overlapping anatomical descriptions with different boundaries. The L13 source diagram labels epicardium, while its bullet text calls pericardium the outermost layer. The heart histology account and pericardial anatomy account support the more precise distinction used here.

An atrium is a receiving chamber; a ventricle is a chamber that ejects into an arterial circuit. The right atrium receives systemic venous return, principally through the superior and inferior venae cavae, and coronary venous return. The right ventricle sends blood toward the lungs. The left atrium receives pulmonary venous blood, and the left ventricle ejects into the aorta. The septum is the tissue partition between right and left chambers. In the normal postnatal arrangement it prevents direct mixing across those partitions; a drawing's apparent overlap is not an additional flow connection.

Four valves connect particular compartments L13 04:03

Figure 5.1 supplies source-labelled anterior, opened and sectioned views of fixed human hearts. These regional observations show great-vessel/RV relationships, pulmonary leaflet location, tricuspid apparatus and septal context; they do not expose all four complete chambers or show live valve motion. Only the anterior view has the source’s anatomical-position designation. In particular, coronary vessels supply the myocardium; blood filling a ventricular chamber does not replace that wall’s own vascular supply.

The tricuspid valve connects right atrium to right ventricle; the mitral, or bicuspid, valve connects left atrium to left ventricle. These are the atrioventricular valves. The pulmonary valve is at the right-ventricular outflow and the aortic valve at the left-ventricular outflow; these are the semilunar valves. A cusp or leaflet is a flexible part of a valve, not merely a sharp edge on a diagram. The NHLBI blood-flow account identifies the four valves and their compartment connections.

Valve leaflets respond to local forces and pressure differences. A chamber's electrical activation does not directly command a leaflet to open. The atrioventricular valves have supporting chordae tendineae, fibrous cords attaching leaflets to ventricular papillary muscles. The support helps oppose leaflet prolapse during ventricular pressure development; papillary contraction does not pull the valve open to initiate filling. Semilunar valves have a different supporting geometry. This distinction matters whenever “the muscles close the valve” is used as a shortcut for the actual pressure-driven sequence.

Let \(P_u\) and \(P_d\) be upstream and downstream pressures across a valve, measured here in mmHg, millimeters of mercury. Let \(R_v\) be a positive hydraulic resistance in mmHg·s/mL. An ideal one-way, or diode, valve obeys

\[Q_v=\frac{\max(P_u-P_d,0)}{R_v}.\tag{5.1}\]

The flow \(Q_v\) is in mL/s and positive in the permitted direction. A positive pressure difference produces forward flow; zero or reverse difference produces zero flow in this idealization. Real leaflets have opening dynamics, inertia, finite closure time and possible leakage; (5.1) resolves none of them. It captures a directional constraint and a testable relation between pressure and allowed flow. An animation using this law must not send a blood marker through a closed valve or through the septum.

The two ventricular pumps are in series L14 02:06

An artery conducts blood away from the heart, and a vein conducts it toward the heart. Oxygen content does not define either term. Systemic arteries usually carry relatively oxygen-rich blood and systemic veins relatively oxygen-poor blood; pulmonary arteries carry blood toward the lungs with lower oxygen content, and pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood. “Deoxygenated” is comparative shorthand, not a claim of zero oxygen. The lecture's general artery/vein description must be read with this pulmonary exception.

The postnatal route is systemic veins → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary arteries → pulmonary microcirculation → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → systemic organs → systemic veins. Pulmonary gas exchange changes blood's gas content; movement into a differently colored heart chamber does not itself oxygenate it. The NHLBI route description provides the anatomical sequence. Coronary circulation is the blood supply of the heart muscle itself, not another name for the whole heart–lung route.

Over a stable repeating interval, the mean right- and left-ventricular outputs must approximately match in this closed-loop organization. A persistent mismatch would accumulate blood in one part of the connected circuit and deplete another. Equality of mean output does not require identical instantaneous ventricular pressure, wall thickness or waveform. The pulmonary and systemic circuits present different loads. For our running monitor case, a regular electrical sequence says little about whether either pump has developed the pressures needed by its circuit.

A cardiac cycle is one repeating sequence of electrical and mechanical events. Ventricular systole includes contraction and ejection; ventricular diastole includes relaxation and filling. The terms must identify the chamber or context: atrial contraction can occur late in ventricular diastole. Pressure development can occur while both ventricular valves are closed. That is an isovolumic interval, approximately constant chamber volume in the ideal model, rather than an interval of absent muscle activity.

The first main heart sound, S1, is associated with atrioventricular valve closure near the beginning of ventricular systole; S2 is associated with aortic and pulmonary valve closure near its end. The NHLBI heartbeat account relates the familiar lub–dub sequence to those closures. Sounds arise from coupled tissue/fluid vibrations, not an ECG wave making an audible noise. An abnormal sound can motivate investigation, but a sound label alone does not measure chamber output or diagnose a specific valve lesion.

5.2 Excitation must be converted into force L13 08:01

A cardiomyocyte is a cardiac muscle cell. Its membrane voltage is a potential difference across the plasma membrane, conventionally inside minus outside. An action potential is a transient, regenerative change in that voltage caused by changing ionic conductances. Electrical coupling between neighboring cardiomyocytes helps propagate excitation; the contracting tissue is composed of cells rather than one literal membrane-bounded giant cell. Propagation coordinates the tissue, while force arises through cellular contractile machinery.

The sarcomere is a repeating contractile unit between two Z discs. Thin filaments include actin and its regulatory proteins; thick filaments contain myosin motors. Z discs anchor thin filaments. The I band contains thin filaments without thick-filament overlap. The A band spans the thick-filament length, including overlapping portions and a central region that can lack thin-filament overlap. Defining the entire A band as only an overlap region, as the source's compressed account tends to do, loses that distinction. Contraction changes filament overlap through sliding; it does not require actin and myosin filaments to shorten into smaller proteins.

Excitation–contraction coupling is the mechanism linking membrane excitation to activation of the contractile proteins. In a working ventricular myocyte, depolarization opens L-type calcium channels in the surface/transverse-tubule membrane. Calcium entering the cytosol helps open type-2 ryanodine receptors in the adjacent sarcoplasmic reticulum, the intracellular calcium-storing membrane network. Calcium release from that store amplifies the cytosolic calcium signal. This is calcium-induced calcium release, described in the directly accessible ryanodine-receptor physiology account.

Calcium binding to the regulatory protein troponin changes access to actin sites, enabling force-producing cross-bridge cycling with myosin. ATP supports the motor cycle and calcium handling. Relaxation requires reducing cytosolic calcium and reducing contractile activation, including uptake through SERCA into the sarcoplasmic reticulum and extrusion through sarcolemmal pathways such as the sodium–calcium exchanger. SERCA is an ATP-driven calcium pump; the exchanger uses coupled ion gradients. Uptake into the store and extrusion from the cell cross different membranes. A cell can therefore have an electrical event while impaired calcium handling, energy supply or myofilament behavior limits force and output.

A working-myocyte action potential is a current balance L13 20:02

For an electrically lumped membrane patch, let \(V_m\) be membrane voltage in volts, \(C_m\) its capacitance in farads and \(I_{\mathrm{ion}}\) total outward-positive membrane ionic current in amperes. Ignoring an externally applied current for this local statement, charge conservation gives

\[C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt}=-I_{\mathrm{ion}}.\tag{5.2}\]

Capacitance converts net current into voltage change. Outward positive charge makes the inside less positive; a net inward positive current has the opposite sign. Equation (5.2) does not say that every active channel has the same current direction or that a nearly level voltage requires all channels to close. A plateau can arise when substantial inward and outward currents nearly balance. A quantitative action-potential model would additionally need individual current laws, channel-state dynamics, ion concentrations and spatial coupling; we do not invent those parameters here.

The conventional fast-response ventricular sequence is labeled phases 0–4. Phase 0 is the rapid upstroke, dominated by inward sodium current through fast voltage-gated sodium channels. Sodium-channel inactivation and a transient outward current contribute to phase 1, the early notch. During phase 2, inward L-type calcium current and outward potassium currents contribute to a prolonged plateau. As the balance shifts toward outward current, phase 3 repolarizes the membrane. Phase 4 is the relatively stable diastolic voltage of the selected working-myocyte account. George's 2013 JCI review, cardiac-action-potential section explicitly describes those competing currents.

The L13 caption's language about separate fast and slow sodium channels, including counting ions per millisecond, is not a sufficient mechanism for the notch and plateau. The supported account includes fast sodium inactivation, transient outward current, L-type calcium influx and several repolarizing potassium currents. Nor does phase number 4 mean that every cardiac cell follows four identical kinetic stages. The numbered phases are a descriptive organization of a particular family of waveforms. Cell type, location, rate and species affect the shape and duration.

The refractory period is an interval of reduced or absent ability to initiate a new propagated response under specified stimulation conditions. Channel recovery and the long working-myocyte waveform help separate successive contractions. A plateau is not a period in which the heart must be mechanically idle. Electrical depolarization, cytosolic calcium and force overlap with delays and different shapes; a plot of one cannot be relabeled as the others without new evidence.

The sinoatrial node supplies a different starting mechanism L13 24:01

The sinoatrial node, SA node, is a specialized pacemaking region in the right atrium near the superior vena cava. Its cells can depolarize spontaneously during diastole. Their diastolic voltage is not the flat phase-4 line of the selected working-ventricular cell. Nodal pacemaking involves coupled membrane-current and intracellular calcium processes; its upstroke relies importantly on calcium currents rather than the same fast-sodium-dominated mechanism used above. The Gordan, Gwathmey and Xie review discusses intrinsic pacing and autonomic modulation. No single imposed resting rate is a universal intrinsic SA-node constant.

The SA node normally leads the intact sequence, while autonomic and hormonal signals alter rate and other cardiac properties. Intrinsic automaticity does not mean the heart ignores the body, and neural influence does not mean each beat requires a new motor command from the brain. This distinction becomes important when interpreting heart-rate changes during exercise, illness or pharmacological intervention.

Excitation spreads through atrial tissue to the atrioventricular node, AV node, at the atrioventricular junction. Relatively slow conduction there helps separate atrial activation from ventricular activation. The impulse continues through the His bundle, bundle branches and Purkinje network to ventricular muscle. These are conducting structures with anatomical routes, not blood vessels transporting electrical particles in the circulation. The NHLBI conduction sequence and heart histology description support this organizing route. Ventricular pressure depends on the subsequent tissue response and load; AV conduction does not itself pump a stroke volume through a valve.

The ECG is a surface electrical measurement L14 20:05

An electrocardiogram, ECG or EKG, records body-surface electrical potential differences associated with cardiac activation. An electrode is a physical contact used to sample potential. A lead is a specified voltage comparison or viewing direction, which can be constructed from several electrode signals. The AHA ECG explanation identifies its electrical nature. The L14 source also explicitly distinguishes electrical activity from subsequent mechanical activity; that correct distinction is retained rather than attributed as an error.

A standard twelve-lead ECG uses ten electrodes: four limb contacts and six chest contacts. Its leads include three standard bipolar limb leads, three augmented limb leads and six precordial leads. The source correctly names those twelve leads but its caption then equates them with twelve sensors. The 2022 ECG review's recording section explicitly distinguishes ten contacts from twelve derived leads. Lead count measures comparisons, not the number of independent physical sensors or the number of cardiac cells being measured.

Define simultaneous electrode potentials \(\phi_{RA}\), \(\phi_{LA}\) and \(\phi_{LL}\), in mV, at right-arm, left-arm and left-leg contacts relative to the same arbitrary reference. The standard bipolar lead voltages are

\[V_I=\phi_{LA}-\phi_{RA},\quad V_{II}=\phi_{LL}-\phi_{RA},\quad V_{III}=\phi_{LL}-\phi_{LA},\qquad V_{II}=V_I+V_{III}.\tag{5.3}\]

The final identity follows by canceling the intermediate left-arm potential. It is a simultaneous voltage identity under consistent polarity, not a statement that three spatially different waveforms must have the same height. A common offset added to all three electrode potentials cancels. The right-leg electrode participates in the recording system's reference/noise-control arrangement; it does not supply a fourth independent bipolar limb-view direction. Figure 5.2 shows the comparison geometry rather than anatomical electrode-placement instructions.

The P wave primarily reflects atrial depolarization, the QRS complex ventricular depolarization and the T wave ventricular repolarization. Atrial repolarization is usually obscured by the larger overlapping QRS signal rather than being a separately resolved extra wave in the simple trace. The PR interval runs from P onset to QRS onset; the QRS duration spans ventricular depolarization; the QT interval runs from QRS onset to T end. The definition uses QRS onset even if that lead has no initial Q deflection. The ST segment lies between QRS end and T onset. These landmarks are supported by the JCI ECG/action-potential comparison and the ECG review; fixed source durations are not adopted as universal normal limits.

A near-flat ST segment does not mean every ventricular cell has returned to resting voltage. It can coexist with widespread depolarization and limited net surface voltage difference. Likewise, a small trace in one lead can reflect projection and cancellation rather than weak muscle force. Measuring an intracellular voltage, a surface lead, a chamber pressure and an ejected volume requires four different measurements. Their relationships constrain interpretation, but none is simply another unit for the same signal. The running low-delivery case consequently requires mechanical and circulatory information in addition to its rhythm strip.

5.3 Output depends on volume, pressure and the connected load L13 12:06

Stroke volume, \(SV\), is the net volume ejected by a ventricle per beat. For a competent-valve cycle without an additional shunt, it is end-diastolic volume minus end-systolic volume: \(SV=V_{ED}-V_{ES}\). Cardiac output, \(CO\), is the mean volume flow delivered by the ventricle. If heart rate \(HR\) is in beats/min and stroke volume in mL/beat, counting ejected volume over successive beats gives

\[CO=HR\,SV,\qquad CO[\mathrm{L/min}]=\frac{HR[\mathrm{beats/min}]\,SV[\mathrm{mL/beat}]}{1000}.\tag{5.4}\]

This is a definition-based accounting relation. It does not tell us how stroke volume changes when rate changes. In the synthetic example, 75 beats/min and 70 mL/beat give 5.25 L/min. At 120 beats/min, preserving 70 mL gives 8.40 L/min, while reducing stroke volume to 35 mL gives 4.20 L/min. A faster rhythm can therefore accompany a lower output. An electrical rate without an ejected-volume estimate cannot resolve the running case.

At 5.25 L/min, one day of turnover is 7560 L. That is recirculated throughput, not the amount of different blood stored inside a person. The lecture's roughly 2000 gallons/day is of this order; if “gallon” means US liquid gallon, the conversion is 7570.823568 L/day or 5.257516 L/min. The conversion convention must be named because gallons are not unique across measurement systems. Neither the lecture scale nor our selected output is an individual physiological reference value.

Ejection fraction, \(EF=SV/V_{ED}\), is the fraction of end-diastolic volume ejected under that accounting. With 120 mL at end diastole and 50 mL at end systole, \(SV=70\) mL and \(EF=0.583333\). The remaining 50 mL does not imply that ejection failed; a functioning ventricle need not empty completely. Conversely, a fraction alone does not specify total output, filling pressure, myocardial work or all forms of heart failure. A fraction and a flow answer different questions.

A prescribed cycle that obeys both conservation and valve pressure inequalities

Let \(T=0.8\) s be the synthetic cycle period and \(x=t/T\) its dimensionless phase between zero and one. Choose \(V_{ES}=50\) mL and \(SV=70\) mL. Atrial inlet pressure is fixed at \(P_A=10\) mmHg and arterial outlet pressure at \(P_O=80\) mmHg. These are ideal pressure reservoirs, not measured atrial and aortic waveforms. The inlet resistance is \(R_{in}=0.01\) and outlet resistance \(R_{out}=0.04\) mmHg·s/mL. Four selected phase fractions are filling 0–0.4, isovolumic contraction 0.4–0.5, ejection 0.5–0.8 and isovolumic relaxation 0.8–1.

For either flowing interval, define local progress \(u\) from zero to one within that interval. Let \(F(u)=(1-\cos\pi u)/2\), a smooth cumulative fraction increasing from zero to one. Its derivative is \(F'(u)=(\pi/2)\sin\pi u\), so flow is nonnegative and vanishes at each interval endpoint. Filling volume is \(V_{ES}+SV\,F(u)\); ejection volume is \(V_{ES}+SV[1-F(u)]\). The two isovolumic intervals hold the respective endpoint volumes. Differentiating these prescribed volumes gives

\[Q_{in}=\frac{SV\pi}{2T_f}\sin(\pi u)\quad\text{during filling},\qquad Q_{out}=\frac{SV\pi}{2T_e}\sin(\pi u)\quad\text{during ejection},\qquad \frac{dV}{dt}=Q_{in}-Q_{out},\tag{5.5}\]

Here \(T_f=0.4T\) and \(T_e=0.3T\) are the filling and ejection durations in seconds. The other flow is zero in each interval. Integration of either sine profile gives exactly \(SV\), so the chamber receives and ejects the same volume over a complete cycle. This establishes periodic mass balance in an incompressible fluid of fixed density. It does not conserve the contents of each ideal reservoir individually; those reservoirs supply and accept the boundary flows.

We now derive ventricular pressure \(P_V\) to satisfy the chosen valve law. During filling, set \(P_V=P_A-R_{in}Q_{in}\). During ejection, set \(P_V=P_O+R_{out}Q_{out}\). During isovolumic contraction, raise \(P_V\) linearly from \(P_A\) to \(P_O\); during isovolumic relaxation, lower it from \(P_O\) to \(P_A\). In both closed intervals, \(P_A\le P_V\le P_O\), so the signed inlet difference \(P_A-P_V\) and outlet difference \(P_V-P_O\) are nonpositive. Equation (5.1) therefore gives zero flow through both valves. At each flowing-interval endpoint, equality across the relevant valve gives zero flow; the exact boundary is not forced open against (5.1).

This construction answers an important consistency question. Volume, flow, pressure and valve state can now be computed from one phase state, and each audits the others. At phase 0.20 the run gives volume 85.000000 mL, ventricular pressure 6.563883 mmHg and inlet flow 343.611696 mL/s. At phase 0.45, volume is 120 mL and pressure 45 mmHg with both flows zero. At phase 0.65, volume is 85 mL, pressure 98.325957 mmHg and outlet flow 458.148929 mL/s. At phase 0.90, volume is 50 mL and pressure 45 mmHg with both flows zero. The two equal 45-mmHg states show why pressure alone need not identify volume or cycle phase.

The physiological organization of filling, closed-valve pressure development, ejection and relaxation is supported by the NHLBI valve account and the cited heart structure/function description. The sine profiles, selected values and linear pressure ramps are original, synthetic choices. We prescribed the stroke volume and derived compatible pressure; we did not predict the stroke volume from muscle mechanics. Doubling \(R_{out}\) to 0.08 leaves the prescribed flow unchanged but raises peak required ventricular pressure to 116.651914 mmHg. A real ventricle may fail to supply that pressure or change its ejection. Predicting that response would require a relation between muscle activation, ventricular pressure and volume, together with a compliant closed circulation.

Blood pressure is not a synonym for flow L14 10:28

Pressure is force per area, not simply force on a vessel. A measured arterial pressure waveform has a systolic maximum and diastolic minimum within the relevant cycle. During ventricular diastole, arterial pressure and flow can persist because compliant arteries store and release volume. Diastole therefore does not mean the entire circulation is at rest or at zero pressure. Our constant outlet reservoir intentionally suppresses that arterial waveform; its 80 mmHg must not be mistaken for both systolic and diastolic pressure in a human.

For a steady resistive segment, let \(\Delta P=P_{up}-P_{down}\) be the pressure drop and \(R\) the resistance. The relation \(Q=\Delta P/R\) defines the selected linear approximation. Using mmHg for pressure and L/min for mean flow makes resistance units mmHg·min/L. A pressure difference of 90 mmHg across resistance 30 gives 3 L/min; across resistance 60 it gives 1.5 L/min. These are synthetic mean-flow cases. A pressure measured at one point cannot determine the driving difference without downstream pressure, and a driving difference cannot determine flow without the load relationship.

In a series path without branches or accumulation, the same mean flow crosses successive segments and pressure drops add, giving \(R_{series}=R_1+R_2\). For parallel branches connecting the same inlet and outlet, pressure drop is shared and flows add, giving \(1/R_{parallel}=1/R_1+1/R_2\). Thus the 30 and 60 branches together have equivalent resistance 20 mmHg·min/L and total flow 4.5 L/min at the selected 90-mmHg drop. These conclusions follow from continuity plus the assumed linear segment law. They do not require equal flow through all organs.

Regional branches and the hepatic portal series L14 28:08

The systemic circulation includes regional beds serving skin, brain, kidneys, skeletal muscle and abdominal organs, among others. Many such beds are arranged as parallel branches between arterial supply and venous return. Their functions differ: skin flow participates in heat exchange, renal flow supports filtration and regulation, and cerebral flow must support neural metabolism. Autoregulation is a tissue's adjustment of its vascular behavior to moderate flow changes as conditions change; it is not perfect flow constancy over every pressure or disease state. Pressure thresholds require a defined bed, subject and measurement, rather than an unqualified plateau drawn through all possible conditions.

Venous return is flow back toward the heart. Veins have substantial storage capacity, and changes in venous tone, surrounding muscle movement and respiratory pressure can affect the distribution and return of blood. The same long-term continuity that links the two ventricles links mean venous return and mean cardiac output in a steady state. Neither can be increased indefinitely while all other stored volumes remain fixed. The regional animation uses labeled routes and a separate fixed-pressure steady network; its moving markers indicate direction and sequence rather than measured transit times.

The splanchnic circulation serves abdominal digestive and associated organs. Much of the blood leaving the gut, spleen and pancreas enters the hepatic portal vein, then traverses liver sinusoids before reaching hepatic veins and the inferior vena cava. A portal arrangement connects two microvascular beds in series. The liver additionally receives hepatic arterial blood. The NASPGHAN Hepatic Physiology teaching material, blood-supply section explicitly describes dual inflow and hepatic venous return.

This corrects several caption shortcuts. The wider abdominal route is splanchnic, rather than all being “splenic,” which refers specifically to the spleen. Hepatic veins return to the inferior vena cava, not an “inferior carotid vein.” The pancreas and the other named organs do not all lie freely inside a single intraperitoneal cavity. The liver has many metabolic and synthetic functions; it does not destroy every toxin or perform all metabolism in the body. The source's sampled routing diagram already places portal flow through the liver before venous return, and that correct series relationship is retained.

Regulation depends on receptor, vascular bed and initial state

The autonomic nervous system regulates cardiac and vascular behavior through sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways. Sympathetic activation can increase cardiac rate, conduction and contractile activation, while parasympathetic vagal influence commonly slows nodal activity. Vascular control is not obtained by copying that cardiac opposition onto every vessel. Most systemic vessels lack extensive parasympathetic innervation, and a decrease in sympathetic constrictor tone can itself permit dilation. The autonomic/endocrine review, autonomic-system and receptor sections directly supports this qualification to the source's general “sympathetic constricts, parasympathetic relaxes” statement.

An adrenergic receptor responds to catecholamine signals such as norepinephrine or epinephrine. Alpha-1 signaling on vascular smooth muscle commonly favors constriction; beta-2 signaling can favor dilation, including responses to circulating epinephrine in relevant beds. The net response depends on receptor distribution, concentration and competing local signals. It is therefore inaccurate to declare that epinephrine and norepinephrine invariably slow every regional flow through one uniform vasoconstrictor action. Even constriction of one branch does not by itself specify the new whole-circuit pressure or output.

The source also names angiotensin II and atrial natriuretic peptide, ANP. Angiotensin II can increase vascular tone and participate in renal salt/water regulation; ANP participates in volume and pressure regulation through vascular and renal actions. These are multiple-site signals rather than instantaneous knobs on a single vessel. Local metabolic activity and endothelial signaling also influence perfusion. The lecture's lactate example should not be interpreted as the sole substance that controls exercise hyperemia, the increased flow associated with active tissue. Demand, oxygen delivery and vascular responses are coupled, and responses differ among beds.

Separate supply failure, structural defects, pump dysfunction and thrombosis

Atherosclerosis is plaque-associated disease of arterial walls; ischemia is inadequate tissue perfusion relative to its requirements. Coronary disease can impair the myocardium's own supply, while coronary plaque disruption and thrombosis can produce acute obstruction. The NHLBI coronary-disease account describes plaque and vessel-function mechanisms. A local narrowed coronary artery can reduce downstream supply; the source's suggested chain “coronary narrowing → pressure builds → stroke” does not adequately locate either the resistance or the injured organ. Stroke concerns brain vascular injury, with ischemic and hemorrhagic mechanisms; myocardial ischemia concerns the heart muscle.

A congenital heart defect is a structural abnormality present at birth. An atrial septal defect connects atrial compartments across a septal opening; a ventricular septal defect connects ventricles. “Arterial septal defect” in the caption is not the atrial lesion being illustrated. A narrowed aorta is another structural-load problem. Each changes the circuit graph or its resistance, so the normal no-shunt mean-output argument requires reconsideration. The NHLBI congenital-defect overview emphasizes walls, valves and major-vessel connections. No uniform oxygenation pattern can be deduced from the word “hole” without pressures, location and flow direction.

Cardiomyopathy is disease of heart muscle that may impair contraction, filling or both, depending on type. A stiff chamber can require a higher filling pressure even when its contraction-related fraction appears preserved. A weak pump can eject less volume despite coordinated electrical activation. The NHLBI cardiomyopathy account describes altered thickness, stiffness or size. Stenosis narrows a valve opening; regurgitation permits backward flow. A resistance-only ideal diode can illustrate a narrowing's additional pressure demand but cannot reproduce regurgitant flow because reverse flow is excluded by construction.

An arrhythmia is an abnormal heart rhythm involving rate, regularity or activation pattern; a change in rate alone does not identify its mechanism. Heart failure describes a clinical syndrome of impaired cardiac performance and associated consequences, not simply the heart having stopped. Cardiac arrest is loss of effective circulation requiring a different emergency framework. The ECG can help characterize electrical activity, but it cannot by itself establish adequate output or exclude a mechanical problem. The synthetic zero-stroke setting deliberately preserves its illustrative electrical annotation to expose that logical distinction, rather than simulate any specific arrest rhythm.

Deep vein thrombosis, DVT, is clot formation in a deep vein. An embolus is material that travels and lodges downstream. A fragment from systemic venous thrombosis can travel through the right heart and obstruct pulmonary arteries, producing pulmonary embolism. The CDC venous-thromboembolism account, dated March 2025, distinguishes those locations and routes. Peripheral arterial disease instead impairs arterial supply to a region. Neither is adequately described by saying any narrow vessel raises all blood pressure everywhere. Topology identifies which downstream tissue or circuit is endangered.

Blood-pressure labels and drug mechanisms have different evidentiary roles L14 14:03

Hypertension denotes persistently elevated arterial pressure under a defined diagnostic framework; hypotension denotes low pressure interpreted in clinical context. The lecture's adult numerical bands and fetal/infant tables are not used as an undifferentiated diagnostic rule. Measurement method, repeated readings, age, gestational context and clinical state matter. A low pressure can accompany inadequate perfusion, but perfusion also depends on downstream pressures and vascular resistance. Conversely, chronic hypertension can damage organs without requiring the immediate rupture of every vessel described by the source.

The AHA fetal-circulation account, reviewed June 2026, describes a circulation with its own pumping heart, placental exchange and specialized shunts. Maternal perfusion of the placenta and fetal circulation are coupled through exchange without being a single freely mixed blood pool. The caption's fetal-pressure values lack enough measurement-site and state information here to serve as reference limits, and its phrase that only after birth the heart generates systolic/diastolic pressure is not adopted. The postnatal two-circuit model in this chapter deliberately does not predict fetal pressure or birth-transition hemodynamics.

The source introduces beta blockers, diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and calcium-channel blockers as pressure-lowering categories. Beta blockers reduce selected beta-adrenergic receptor responses, including cardiac responses. Diuretics alter renal salt and water handling. ACE converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II; inhibiting that conversion reduces angiotensin-II formation and its contribution to vascular constriction and salt/water regulation. Calcium-channel blockers affect selected excitable and vascular tissues. The AHA medication-class explanation, directly checked in November 2026, states that some calcium-channel blockers also reduce heart rate. The class must not be equated with uniform rate slowing in every product. These targets explain the lecture categories without prescribing a treatment, dose, drug hierarchy or monitoring schedule.

5.4 Reproduce the model and challenge its assumptions

The dependency-free cardiac-cycle program implements the selected volume profile, its analytic flow, pressure construction, ideal diode tests and regional network. Inputs use phase 0–1, stroke volume in mL, period in seconds, pressure in mmHg and resistance in the stated time convention. It returns phase state and prints outputs. Invalid phase, negative stroke volume, zero resistance, nonfinite inputs and improperly ordered reservoir pressures are rejected. It reads no patient data, writes no files and uses no network.

python code/ch05_cardiac_cycle.py

The executed run checked 1001 cycle states, inlet/outlet diode inequalities and mutual exclusivity, finite-difference volume balance away from phase boundaries, periodic volume, resistance scaling, equal/reverse-pressure zero flow and the zero-stroke limit. Midpoint integration over 10000 intervals gave 70.000002 mL inflow and 70.000003 mL outflow, against the analytic 70 mL for each. The tiny positive integration discrepancies are quadrature error; they are not fluid created by the analytic model. These checks establish implementation consistency, not agreement with a human pressure–volume recording.

For the separate regional example, arterial and venous reservoirs are 95 and 5 mmHg. Gut and hepatic-artery resistances meet at one liver inlet pressure, with hepatic outflow resistance downstream. Applying the same linear flow relation at that junction requires hepatic outflow to equal the two incoming flows. The baseline chosen values give portal inflow 1.2 L/min, hepatic arterial inflow 0.3 L/min and hepatic venous outflow 1.5 L/min. Kidney and other branches add 1.0 and 2.75 L/min, giving total 5.25 L/min. These are selected synthetic branch values, not human perfusion fractions. Changing the hepatic resistance recalculates the common inlet pressure and both inflows; holding all three old flows fixed while changing the pressure would violate the network equations.

A predictive pump model would require muscle activation, passive pressure–volume behavior, ventricular interaction, compliant arteries and veins, and closed-loop volume redistribution. A predictive electrophysiology model would require ionic currents and tissue propagation; a forward ECG model would also require cardiac sources and body conduction geometry. A regurgitation model must permit backward flow, and a shunt model must add the missing connection. Those are concrete extensions, rather than defects repaired by adding realistic-looking motion to the current abstraction.

5.5 Six problems with worked solutions

Problem 5.1. Repair a route and a wall description

Anatomical reasoning; section 5.1. A diagram labels the outer heart-wall layer “pericardium,” sends oxygen-rich blood through the pulmonary artery to the left atrium, and opens the mitral valve whenever an electrical P wave appears. Repair each claim and trace a systemic venous parcel through both ventricular circuits. State which parts of that route change its oxygenation.

Worked solution

The wall layers are endocardium, myocardium and epicardium; the pericardium is the surrounding sac, with the epicardial surface associated with its visceral serous layer. Pulmonary arteries leave the right ventricle toward the lungs and usually carry relatively oxygen-poor blood. Pulmonary veins return from the lungs to the left atrium. Direction, rather than oxygen content, determines artery versus vein.

The route is vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary arteries → lung exchange bed → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → systemic exchange bed → systemic veins. Pulmonary gas exchange adds oxygen to the blood; systemic tissue exchange removes some of it. Merely crossing a valve changes location, not oxygen content by itself. The mitral valve opens when the relevant left-atrial-to-ventricular pressure difference favors forward flow; a P wave records atrial depolarization and cannot independently establish that pressure difference.

Problem 5.2. Detect an impossible animated beat

Conservation and inequality audit; sections 5.1 and 5.3. A model increases ventricular volume while both valve flows are zero. At another instant it sends 5 mL/s through an ideal outlet valve while ventricular pressure is below outlet pressure. Identify the independent violations. Then explain why two phases with the same pressure can have different volume and valve histories.

Worked solution

Without a shunt or a moving control-volume definition, the chamber balance is \(dV/dt=Q_{in}-Q_{out}\). Zero flows require zero volume derivative; an increasing volume creates fluid in the model. The outlet claim separately violates (5.1): with \(P_V-P_O\lt0\), the ideal-diode flow must be zero. Correcting only the amount balance would not repair that valve-direction error.

In our executed cycle, phase 0.45 has 45 mmHg at 120 mL, while phase 0.90 has 45 mmHg at 50 mL. The former is pressure rising at end-diastolic volume; the latter is pressure falling at end-systolic volume. Both have zero inlet/outlet flow, but their preceding and following states differ. Pressure is therefore not a one-to-one code for volume or for phase. A pressure–volume trajectory preserves history that one instantaneous number discards.

Problem 5.3. Test the required-pressure claim

Computational model criticism; section 5.3. Run the cycle with outlet resistance 0.04 and 0.08 mmHg·s/mL, keeping the prescribed volume trajectory fixed. Compare the peak required pressure. A colleague says the result proves that doubling valve resistance does not affect a living person's stroke volume. Diagnose the inference and specify the missing model ingredient.

Worked solution

At peak ejection flow, phase 0.65, the program returns 458.148929 mL/s. Required pressure is \(80+R_{out}Q_{out}\). The two resistances give 98.325957 and 116.651914 mmHg. The excess above the fixed outlet reservoir doubles because flow was held fixed, while the reservoir's baseline pressure was unchanged.

The construction takes stroke volume as an input. It cannot use an imposed unchanged stroke volume as evidence that real stroke volume is insensitive to resistance. A predictive extension needs a pressure-generating/contractile relation coupled to chamber volume and the downstream load; the chamber must then be allowed to fail to eject the imposed amount or alter the time course. With that extension, both pressure and volume become outcomes. The current result is a consistency/required-pressure calculation, not a clinical valve-stenosis experiment.

Problem 5.4. Separate rate, output and ejection fraction

Regime comparison; section 5.3. Compare 75 beats/min with 70 mL/beat against 120 beats/min with 35 mL/beat. Does a faster electrical rhythm prove better delivery? For an end-diastolic/end-systolic pair 120/50 mL, compute the ejection fraction and explain why that number cannot identify all causes of low delivery.

Worked solution

The first output is \(75\times70/1000=5.25\) L/min. The second is \(120\times35/1000=4.20\) L/min, so the faster rhythm has lower output in these synthetic cases. A rate rise does not specify changes in filling time, end-diastolic filling, muscle contraction or ejection. The alternative 120/min with a retained 70-mL stroke would instead give 8.40 L/min; the program reproduces all three regimes.

The selected fraction is \((120-50)/120=0.583333\). It supplies neither heart rate nor absolute end-diastolic volume in another person, and does not measure downstream distribution, oxygen content or filling pressure. Two people with the same fraction can have different output and regional delivery. A normal-looking fraction or regular rhythm cannot, on its own, close the running monitor case.

Problem 5.5. Derive a lead identity and an interpretation limit

Derivation and counterexample; section 5.2. Starting with electrode potentials, derive the relation among standard leads I, II and III. What happens if the same offset is added to every limb potential? Would an electrically near-flat ST segment prove zero intracellular depolarization or zero ventricular force?

Worked solution

Adding \(V_I=\phi_{LA}-\phi_{RA}\) to \(V_{III}=\phi_{LL}-\phi_{LA}\) cancels \(\phi_{LA}\) and leaves \(\phi_{LL}-\phi_{RA}=V_{II}\). Adding a common offset to all electrode potentials cancels within every difference, so none of the three voltages changes. This assumes simultaneous measurements with consistent polarity and reference conventions; a lead is a comparison, not an extra sensor.

A surface lead depends on the spatial distribution of electrical sources and the comparison geometry. Widespread depolarized tissue can produce little net lead voltage difference, so the ST counterexample defeats the proposed inference about resting intracellular voltage. Mechanical force depends additionally on calcium handling, myofilaments, loading and other factors; it is not measured by a flat surface segment. Ten electrodes can generate twelve standard leads because the comparisons are derived, not twelve independent physical contacts.

Problem 5.6. Repair a portal-flow model and a vascular shortcut

Network design and assumption diagnosis; section 5.3. A simulation sends gut venous blood directly to the vena cava while labeling it portal return. It adds hepatic arterial inflow to the liver but leaves hepatic outflow equal only to gut inflow. It then declares that epinephrine must reduce every organ's flow. Correct the topology, junction balance and regulatory conclusion.

Worked solution

The portal route connects gut/splenic drainage to liver sinusoids before hepatic venous return to the inferior vena cava. Sending it directly to the systemic venous collector omits the second microvascular bed. At steady state without storage or other branches, liver outflow equals portal inflow plus hepatic arterial inflow. In the selected network that is \(1.2+0.3=1.5\) L/min; omitting the arterial contribution would accumulate blood or destroy the junction balance. Changing an outflow resistance requires recalculating the shared pressure and both inflows together.

Adrenergic action depends on receptor and vascular bed, along with dose and other signals. Alpha-1-mediated constriction and beta-2-mediated dilation provide distinct possibilities; cardiac effects can also change the driving pressure. A uniform “every flow decreases” conclusion ignores both local response and the connected network. The proper next step is to specify which receptor/bed changes which resistance, then solve the resulting pressure and flow system under explicit boundary conditions. Neither the lecture slogan nor a colored arrow supplies that missing model.
