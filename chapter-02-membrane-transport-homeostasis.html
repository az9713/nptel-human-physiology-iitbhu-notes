02. Membrane transport and homeostasis

A membrane can remain physically intact while its cell loses the ability to regulate its contents. Imagine an absorptive epithelial cell supplied with glucose on one side and connected to tissue fluid on the other. It needs selective entry, an exit route, energy to maintain ion differences, and signals that adjust transport to demand. Making the membrane freely permeable would destroy some of the differences that power absorption. Making it completely impermeable would stop absorption. The useful question is which substance crosses which boundary, under which driving force, and how the cell replaces the energy that transport dissipates.

Source spine: official lectures L4, Ion & Molecular Transport I, L5, Ion & Molecular Transport II, and L6, Homeostasis and Diseases caused due to Malfunction of Cell Organelles, Sudip Mukherjee, IIT (BHU) Varanasi. Captions establish sequence; a separate sampled-still review resolves selected slide labels, without establishing every spoken statement. Mechanistic corrections use independently checked sources. The compartment equations, energy examples, feedback model, diagrams and code are original teaching constructions. Their numerical inputs are synthetic. The Nernst calculation and NCX1 experiment are labeled extensions.

2.1 A selective boundary needs a balance equation L4 00:26

The plasma membrane separates a cell's interior from its immediate external environment. Intracellular fluid is fluid inside cells; extracellular fluid is fluid outside them. The latter includes blood plasma and interstitial fluid, the fluid between tissue cells. These compartments communicate but are not identical. An intestinal lumen is a further, distinct compartment: a substance in the gut lumen has not yet crossed the epithelium into the body's internal fluid.

For our running example, distinguish lumen, epithelial-cell cytosol, and interstitial fluid. Cytosol means the aqueous cellular region outside membrane-bounded organelles. The cell's apical membrane faces the lumen; its basolateral membrane faces neighboring cells and underlying interstitial tissue. The distribution of transport proteins between these surfaces is part of epithelial polarity. A channel drawn anywhere on a circle is insufficient to explain directed absorption: a route across one membrane must connect to an appropriate route across the other.

Let \(n_i\) denote the amount of a selected substance in compartment \(i\), in moles; \(V_i\) its volume, in cubic meters; and \(c_i=n_i/V_i\) its concentration, in mol/m³. A millimole per liter, mmol/L, has the same numerical value as a mole per cubic meter. Let \(F_{ji}\) denote amount transported from compartment \(j\) into \(i\) per second, in mol/s, and \(S_i\) net production of that substance by chemical reactions, also in mol/s. With time \(t\) measured in seconds, conservation gives

\[ \frac{dn_i}{dt}=\sum_j(F_{ji}-F_{ij})+S_i. \tag{2.1} \]

The sign convention is explicit: inward transport contributes positively. A substance that leaves one compartment enters another unless the model includes an external boundary or reaction. If volume is fixed, \(dn_i/dt=V_i\,dc_i/dt\). If water movement changes volume, the correct relation is \(dn_i/dt=V_i\,dc_i/dt+c_i\,dV_i/dt\). Thus a rising concentration can reflect net solute entry, net water loss, or both. The amount balance must be established before interpreting a concentration trace.

Equation (2.1) does not select a transport mechanism. It specifies the accounting that every mechanism must respect. A membrane pump changes a transport rate; a metabolic enzyme changes a reaction rate; a hormone can alter either through signaling. The rest of the chapter supplies constitutive laws, meaning relations that specify rates in terms of driving forces and properties of the system. Their assumptions determine when they can be used.

2.2 Permeability is a property of a substance and a pathway L4 06:07

A phospholipid bilayer has polar head groups facing aqueous fluid on both sides and nonpolar hydrocarbon regions facing inward. Moving a hydrated ion into this hydrophobic interior is energetically unfavorable. The barrier is not adequately explained as polar heads repelling every polar substance: hydration, charge, lipid solubility and the interior environment matter. Small nonpolar gases can cross the lipid phase far more readily than typical inorganic ions. Cooper, cell membranes, describes this selective-permeability principle; the directly readable material here was its indexed excerpt.

Simple diffusion refers here to passive passage through the lipid phase without a transport protein. Facilitated diffusion uses a membrane protein while proceeding down the transported substance's electrochemical driving force. A channel provides a selective aqueous pathway when open. A carrier binds a substrate and changes conformation so that its binding region is exposed to opposite sides in sequence. An open channel is not an indefinitely open carrier, and a carrier is not necessarily an active pump. Protein participation and energy coupling are separate classification axes. Alberts et al., principles of membrane transport, distinguishes the routes.

For the epithelial case, selective glucose entry cannot be inferred from the permeability of oxygen. Different substances can use different routes through the same membrane. Nor does raising transporter abundance make the membrane freely permeable to everything. A useful experiment changes one route while measuring a named substance on both sides. If glucose transport changes while an unrelated ion flux does not, that is evidence for pathway specificity rather than wholesale barrier failure.

Water provides another warning about categories. Osmosis is water movement driven by a water chemical-potential difference across a selectively permeable boundary. Aquaporins are water-channel proteins; water can also have a lipid-mediated route. Osmolarity counts osmotically active particles per liter of solution; osmolality counts them per kilogram of solvent. Tonicity concerns the sustained effect of an external solution on cell volume and depends on effective, poorly penetrating solutes. Equal total osmolarity need not mean equal tonicity if one solute enters the cell. These definitions prevent applying a fixed-volume solute model to a cell that swells. The transport overview in OpenStax §3.1 supplies introductory context; no figure from it is reproduced.

2.3 From a spatial gradient to a transport rate L4 12:12

Diffusion is dispersal produced by random molecular motion. A concentration difference produces a net flux even though individual molecules continue crossing both ways. Net zero at equilibrium does not mean that every molecule stops. The ink-in-water demonstration near L4 28:03 motivates dispersal, but it is not a membrane-permeability measurement: bulk stirring and density-driven motion may contribute in an actual beaker.

For an uncharged, dilute solute, assume one-dimensional steady diffusion through a uniform planar layer. Let \(x\) increase from the external side to the internal side, let \(D>0\) be diffusivity in m²/s, and let \(J\) be inward flux density in mol/(m²·s). Fick's constitutive law is \(J=-D\,dc/dx\). The negative sign means that the net flux follows decreasing concentration. If the layer thickness is \(\ell>0\), the concentration profile is linear, and its boundary concentrations are \(c_o\) outside and \(c_i\) inside, integrating the constant gradient gives

\[ J=\frac{D}{\ell}(c_o-c_i),\qquad F=A J. \tag{2.2} \]

Here \(A\) is exchange area in m² and \(F\) total inward transport in mol/s. A lipid membrane adds partitioning between water and lipid. If \(K\) is the dimensionless equilibrium ratio of membrane-phase to aqueous concentration at each interface, the membrane boundary concentrations become \(Kc_o\) and \(Kc_i\). Define permeability \(p=DK/\ell\), in m/s, and conductance \(g=Ap\), in m³/s. Then \(F=g(c_o-c_i)\). The same algebra is valid using liters for volume and mmol/L for concentration if \(g\) is expressed in L/s; the resulting transport rate is mmol/s.

The consequential step is separating geometry from material behavior. Increasing area at fixed permeability increases total transfer without changing flux density. Increasing thickness reduces permeability only if diffusivity and partitioning remain unchanged. There is no universal inverse-linear law between molecular weight and membrane flux: molecular shape, hydration, charge, partitioning and protein pathways can dominate. The lecture's qualitative size comparison should not be promoted to \(F\propto1/\text{molecular weight}\) for arbitrary biological solutes.

For a synthetic example choose \(g=0.1\ \mathrm{L/s}\) and concentrations 10 and 0 mmol/L. Initial inward transfer is 1 mmol/s. Doubling area alone doubles \(g\) and hence transfer. Adding 10 mmol/L to both sides changes the pair to 20 and 10 mmol/L, preserves their difference, and leaves this linear prediction at 1 mmol/s. Doubling both original concentrations instead gives 20 and 0 mmol/L and doubles transfer to 2 mmol/s. A rate law depending only on the concentration difference need not describe a saturating carrier. Equation (2.2) also omits voltage, pressure-driven flow, reactions within the layer and unstirred boundary layers. It is a model for a selected passive route, not the rate law of every membrane protein.

2.4 A closed pair equilibrates by conserving amount

Now let the same passive route connect two well-mixed, fixed-volume compartments without production or removal. Let their volumes be \(V_o\) and \(V_i\), in liters, and their concentrations be \(c_o(t)\) and \(c_i(t)\), in mmol/L. The subscripts denote outside and inside. Assume \(g\) constant and no water movement. Combining the flux law with (2.1) gives

\[ V_i\frac{dc_i}{dt}=g(c_o-c_i),\qquad V_o\frac{dc_o}{dt}=-g(c_o-c_i). \tag{2.3} \]

Adding the equations proves that \(V_oc_o+V_ic_i\) is constant. To derive the time dependence, define \(\Delta=c_o-c_i\). Subtract the two concentration derivatives, taking care that the same amount produces different concentration changes in unequal volumes:

\[ \frac{d\Delta}{dt}=-g\left(\frac1{V_o}+\frac1{V_i}\right)\Delta, \quad \Delta(t)=\Delta(0)e^{-t/\tau},\quad \tau=\frac1{g(1/V_o+1/V_i)}. \tag{2.4} \]

The time constant \(\tau\) is the time over which the concentration difference falls by a factor \(e\), where \(e\) is the base of natural logarithms. Equilibrium is the volume-weighted concentration \(\bar c=[V_oc_o(0)+V_ic_i(0)]/(V_o+V_i)\). Solving the conservation and difference equations together yields

\[ c_o(t)=\bar c+\frac{V_i}{V_o+V_i}\Delta(t),\qquad c_i(t)=\bar c-\frac{V_o}{V_o+V_i}\Delta(t). \tag{2.5} \]

Take synthetic values \(V_o=2\) L, \(V_i=1\) L, \(g=0.1\) L/s, \(c_o(0)=10\) and \(c_i(0)=0\) mmol/L. The total is 20 mmol, the equilibrium concentration is 6.666667 mmol/L, the time constant is 6.666667 s, and the difference half-time is 4.620981 s. At 10 s, outside is 7.410434 and inside 5.179132 mmol/L. Multiplying by volumes returns 20 mmol. Averaging the two initial concentrations without volume weights would incorrectly predict 5 mmol/L.

This model explains why passive uptake slows in a closed preparation. The gradient that powers uptake is consumed by uptake. A living absorptive cell instead exchanges with replenished luminal fluid, metabolizes substrates, and connects to circulating extracellular fluid. Treating one side as an infinite reservoir may then be appropriate over a limited time, but it changes the balance and time constant. A maintained difference is evidence that a closed equilibration model is missing a process; it is not evidence that diffusion has ceased.

Static and print alternative: in the closed diffusion example, the concentration pairs (outside, inside) are (10, 0) at 0 s; (8.241222, 3.517556) at 5 s; (7.410434, 5.179132) at 10 s; and (6.832624, 6.334753) at 20 s, in mmol/L. Transfer remains inward and diminishes; total amount stays 20 mmol. An impermeable boundary preserves the initial pair.

2.5 Gates change access; driving forces determine direction L4 16:05

A leak channel contributes a resting permeability, though the term does not imply that every such protein is permanently open. A voltage-gated channel changes its opening probability in response to membrane voltage. A ligand-gated channel responds to binding of a chemical ligand, and a mechanically gated channel responds to mechanical forces. The stimulus that opens a channel is distinct from the energy that moves its permeant ions. A ligand may open a route without being transported through it.

Selectivity and gating are also distinct. Opening a sodium-selective channel does not imply that calcium can enter through that channel at the same rate. Nor is a voltage such as −55 mV a universal switch for every voltage-gated protein. Different channel types have distinct voltage dependence, kinetics and ion selectivity. Both sides of a channel contact the two fluid compartments: “outside sodium channels” and “inside potassium channels” are misleading if they imply that different channels occupy only one membrane face. Alberts et al., ion channels, supplies the channel/electrochemical framework; direct access returned a browser check, so the indexed excerpts were used only for bounded corroboration.

For charged solutes, a concentration comparison is insufficient. Define the membrane potential \(V_m=\phi_i-\phi_o\), the electrical potential inside relative to outside, in volts. Define \(z\) as the signed ion valence, for example +1 for Na⁺ and +2 for Ca²⁺. In an ideal dilute solution at absolute temperature \(T\), the free-energy change per mole for transfer outside → inside is

\[ \Delta G_{o\to i}=RT\ln\!\left(\frac{c_i}{c_o}\right)+z\mathcal F V_m. \tag{2.6} \]

Here \(R=8.31446261815324\) J/(mol·K) is the gas constant, \(\mathcal F=96485.33212\) C/mol is the rounded Faraday constant, and \(\ln\) denotes the natural logarithm. Concentrations must be positive and use the same unit; their ratio is dimensionless. \(\mathcal F\) is distinguished from the transport rate \(F\). A joule per mole is the unit of both terms because coulombs times volts are joules. This is a labeled thermodynamic extension of the lecture's gradient language. It assumes ideal activities and omits additional coupled reactions.

The chemical term is negative for inward movement from high outside concentration to low inside concentration. A negative inside voltage makes the electrical term negative for a cation. Both effects therefore favor sodium entry in the usual qualitative example. A negative total \(\Delta G\) means that the specified transfer is energetically downhill; positive means uphill. A channel can provide a pathway, but opening it does not change the sign rule into “always inward.” Zero driving force also does not imply zero channel conductance.

As a synthetic calculation at \(T=310\) K, set sodium concentrations inside/outside to 15/145 mmol/L and \(V_m=-0.070\) V. Inward free energy is −12.601467 kJ/mol. For potassium with 140 mmol/L inside and 4 mmol/L outside at the same voltage, inward free energy is +2.409877 kJ/mol; outward transfer is favored. These are selected illustrative concentrations, not universal normal values for all cells. Even for ions with these concentrations, the amount transferred per second still requires permeability and kinetic information.

A single-ion equilibrium is a conditional result

Setting (2.6) to zero and solving for voltage defines the ion's equilibrium potential \(E\):

\[ E=\frac{RT}{z\mathcal F}\ln\!\left(\frac{c_o}{c_i}\right). \tag{2.7} \]

This is the Nernst relation. For the selected potassium ratio it gives −94.976619 mV, where a millivolt is one thousandth of a volt. Substituting that voltage into (2.6) returns zero within numerical precision. A multi-ion membrane generally does not sit at every ion's equilibrium potential simultaneously. The resting membrane potential requires relative permeabilities and current balance, and the concentrations themselves require maintained transport. The Neuroscience discussion of membrane forces supports the equilibrium interpretation, rather than the claim that a Nernst calculation alone predicts every resting cell.

2.6 A carrier can saturate without pumping uphill L4 26:06

A carrier's binding and conformational steps take time. Increasing substrate concentration initially increases occupancy, but a finite population of carriers cannot complete arbitrarily many cycles per second. To isolate this distinction, consider a one-way uptake assay in which internal substrate is kept low. Let \(c\) be external concentration, \(F_{\max}\) the limiting uptake rate in amount/time, and \(K_m\) the concentration at half that rate. A simple saturating teaching law is

\[ F(c)=F_{\max}\frac{c}{K_m+c}. \tag{2.8} \]

This follows from a rapid-binding occupancy model: the occupied fraction is \(c/(K_m+c)\), and the completion rate is proportional to occupied carriers. The denominator is essential. At \(c\ll K_m\), uptake is approximately linear with slope \(F_{\max}/K_m\); at \(c\gg K_m\), it approaches \(F_{\max}\). Increasing carrier number can raise \(F_{\max}\) without changing the concentration ratio at equilibrium. The construction is not a general reversible carrier law: using it to predict net inward transport when inside and outside concentrations are equal would be a misuse of the one-way assay assumption.

GLUT4 is a glucose transporter, not an insulin receptor and not a sodium–glucose cotransporter. Insulin signaling can increase its abundance at the plasma membrane in relevant muscle and fat cells by redistributing intracellular transporter-containing vesicles. The substrate can then undergo facilitated diffusion. ATP expenditure on trafficking and signaling does not make each glucose crossing through GLUT4 an ATP-coupled uphill event. In an original 1992 permeabilized-adipocyte experiment, investigators measured GLUT4 movement and tested nucleotide requirements. The preparation was cultured 3T3-L1 cells; it was not a whole-human glucose-control experiment.

For the running epithelial cell, importing glucose through a sodium-coupled route at one surface and releasing it through a GLUT-family route at the other are different tasks. The first can accumulate glucose against its own gradient; the second can allow downhill exit once the cell has accumulated it. Calling both proteins “glucose channels” hides the difference that determines whether absorption can be sustained. The next section specifies where the energy for accumulation comes from.

2.7 Primary active transport maintains a nonequilibrium state L5 02:07

Active transport moves a substance uphill in electrochemical free energy by coupling it to an energetically favorable process. Primary active transport couples transport directly to a chemical or other primary energy source. An ATPase is an enzyme that hydrolyzes adenosine triphosphate, ATP, to adenosine diphosphate, ADP, and inorganic phosphate. Hydrolysis has an available free-energy change that depends on the chemical conditions, including the concentrations of ATP, ADP and phosphate. It is not a universal fixed allotment regardless of cell state.

The Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase transports three Na⁺ out of the cytosol and two K⁺ into it per ATP hydrolyzed in its usual forward cycle. Cytosolic sodium binding, phosphorylation, changes in access, release of the bound sodium into extracellular fluid and extracellular potassium binding, dephosphorylation, and internal potassium release form a cyclic mechanism. This sequence supports directionality through coupled chemical transitions, rather than through a permanently open conduit. The net movement of one positive charge outward per cycle makes the pump electrogenic. Maintaining the gradients also supports many other currents indirectly. Alberts et al., carrier proteins and active transport, describes the cycle and gradient coupling; only indexed excerpts were readable during this preparation.

For an energetic check, let \(\Delta G_{\rm Na,in}\) and \(\Delta G_{\rm K,in}\) be the inward molar energies given by (2.6). Reversing a transfer reverses its free-energy change. The transport work per mole of forward pump cycles is therefore

\[ W_{\rm pump}=-3\Delta G_{\rm Na,in}+2\Delta G_{\rm K,in}. \tag{2.9} \]

For the selected concentrations and −70 mV, this is 42.624157 kJ/mol of cycles. If \(\Delta G_{\rm ATP}\) denotes the actual molar free energy of ATP hydrolysis, a necessary condition for the coupled forward cycle is \(W_{\rm pump}+\Delta G_{\rm ATP}<0\). A negative sum permits forward operation thermodynamically; it does not determine turnover rate. The inequality explains why sufficiently depleted chemical energy or altered gradients can compromise pumping without a hole appearing in the membrane.

The static sequence corresponding to the pump animation is: bind three cytosolic sodium ions; use ATP-linked phosphorylation to change access and release them outside; bind two extracellular potassium ions; dephosphorylate and return access inward; release potassium to the cytosol. The phase durations in the asset are teaching intervals, not measured dwell times. A sodium-dependent transporter is drawn separately because it does not hydrolyze that ATP in its own coupled transport step.

The lecture also introduces hormonal modulation. Insulin and thyroid-related signaling can influence pump activity and abundance in tissue- and timescale-dependent ways; this introductory chapter does not assign one universal gain to those effects. Increasing ATP-consuming transport can contribute to energetic demand, but thyroid effects on heat production cannot be reduced to a single pump. The central distinction for the epithelial case is direct ATP coupling at the Na/K pump versus indirect energy dependence elsewhere.

A calcium pump crosses a different boundary L5 12:06

The sarcoplasmic reticulum, abbreviated SR, is a specialized endoplasmic-reticulum compartment in muscle. Its lumen is inside the cell but distinct from the cytosol. SERCA, the sarco/endoplasmic-reticulum Ca²⁺ ATPase, uses ATP to transfer calcium from cytosol into that lumen. Reducing free cytosolic calcium contributes to muscle relaxation. Cytosolic calcium does not become literally zero, and “sarcoplasm” names muscle-cell cytoplasm rather than the SR storage lumen.

Two arrows both pointing away from cytosol can therefore have different destinations. Calcium extrusion across the plasma membrane removes calcium from the cell; calcium uptake by SERCA stores it within the cell. In a cytosolic balance, both can be removal terms. In a whole-cell calcium balance, only extrusion is loss from the cell. This is a useful test of whether the compartment boundary in a model matches the question being asked.

2.8 Secondary transport spends an ion gradient L5 16:02

Secondary active transport couples an uphill transfer to the downhill transfer of another substance. A symporter transports coupled substances in the same direction across a membrane; an antiporter exchanges them in opposite directions. These labels specify relative directions, not energy sources by themselves. A sodium–glucose symporter can use inward sodium movement to drive glucose accumulation. Sodium must subsequently be removed to sustain the driving force, commonly through basolateral Na/K ATPase activity.

Let \(m\) be the number of sodium ions coupled to one glucose molecule, a positive integer. Treat glucose as electrically neutral, with concentrations \(c_{G,i}\) and \(c_{G,o}\). In an ideal, tightly coupled inward cycle, summing the free-energy changes gives

\[ \Delta G_{\rm cycle}=m\Delta G_{\rm Na,in} +RT\ln(c_{G,i}/c_{G,o}). \tag{2.10} \]

Net inward cycling is energetically favored when the sum is negative, even if the glucose term alone is positive. At the ideal reversible limit, solve for the glucose concentration ratio:

\[ \left(\frac{c_{G,i}}{c_{G,o}}\right)_{\max} =\exp\!\left[-\frac{m\Delta G_{\rm Na,in}}{RT}\right]. \tag{2.11} \]

The subscript “max” marks a thermodynamic ceiling under the model assumptions. It is not an achieved steady-state ratio in tissue. Backleak, kinetics, metabolism, competing currents and changing sodium concentration may keep a real cell far below it. The derivation also assumes voltage held fixed by processes outside this minimal model. It is not self-consistent to import unlimited charge into an electrically isolated cell while asserting its voltage never changes.

Use synthetic sodium concentrations 15/145 mmol/L, \(T=310\) K and \(V_m=-60\) mV. Inward sodium energy is −11.636614 kJ/mol. A tenfold uphill glucose ratio requires 5.934875 kJ/mol. One coupled sodium ion can therefore make that selected cycle favorable. The ideal ratio ceilings are 91.351921 for \(m=1\) and 8345.173388 for \(m=2\). Squaring the first ceiling gives the second: coupling stoichiometry enters exponentially, so it must never be guessed from arrow counts in a cartoon.

The physiological architecture supplies the missing boundary conditions. Luminal uptake through sodium–glucose cotransport, basolateral glucose exit, and basolateral sodium extrusion permit directed transepithelial transport. The IUPHAR/BPS transporter database locates SGLT2 predominantly in early proximal renal tubule and distinguishes it from SGLT1. Here the stoichiometric calculation is deliberately generic rather than an asserted fit of either protein.

The renal examples in L5 include SGLT inhibition, Na/K/2Cl cotransport and Na/H exchange. Their teaching role is to show that distinct proteins couple different solutes and occupy particular epithelial surfaces and segments. SGLT inhibition reduces a glucose-reabsorption route; loop diuretics act on a different transport system; Na/H exchange is secondary transport rather than a directly ATP-hydrolyzing pump. The IUPHAR/BPS entries for SGLT2, NKCC2 and sodium/hydrogen exchangers supply bounded indexed target/nomenclature checks. A “pump” label on a slide does not establish direct ATP coupling. Detailed segmental physiology and drug indications require their later renal and clinical contexts, and are not inferred from the schematic. No current treatment hierarchy or dosing claim is made here.

2.9 Calcium handling exposes the cost of a mislabeled arrow L5 28:28

NCX denotes sodium–calcium exchange. In a typical forward extrusion mode at the plasma membrane of a cardiac muscle cell, sodium enters while calcium leaves the cytosol for extracellular fluid. Its operation depends on gradients, voltage and regulatory state; exchange can reverse under suitable conditions. SERCA crosses the SR membrane instead. Calling NCX the transporter that directly places calcium into SR confuses plasma membrane extrusion with intracellular sequestration.

Digoxin inhibits Na/K ATPase. Reduced sodium extrusion tends to raise intracellular sodium, weakening the sodium gradient that supports calcium extrusion through NCX. More calcium can remain available for intracellular cycling, including SERCA-mediated SR uptake and later release, increasing contractile force in an appropriate cardiac context. This is a coupled system: inhibiting one pump can alter a different transported species without binding a glucose transporter or opening a calcium pore. The DailyMed digoxin label, sections 1 and 12.1, directly supports Na/K ATPase inhibition, increased intracellular calcium availability, and selected heart-failure and atrial-fibrillation indications. These statements were checked on 2026-11-04.

Source correction: the sampled L5 printed slide at 32:00 says heart failure; caption passages say cardiac arrest. Without full audio inspection, the caption/slide discrepancy must remain attributed to its actual evidence types. Heart failure and cardiac arrest are distinct conditions, and the manuscript does not present digoxin as cardiac-arrest treatment. The same slide's claim that pump inhibition lowers sodium concentration is incorrect if it means intracellular sodium in the proposed mechanism. The relevant initial change is reduced sodium extrusion and a tendency for intracellular sodium to rise. No inference about prescribing follows from the mechanism.

Research extension: thermodynamics does not replace protein regulation

An original study tested a finer question: is changing the sodium gradient alone sufficient to explain digoxin’s effect? Scranton et al., Science Advances (2025), DOI 10.1126/sciadv.ady9596, compared wild-type mice with mice carrying a mutation in NCX1, the sodium–calcium exchanger isoform studied. Sodium-dependent inactivation is a reduction of exchanger activity triggered by intracellular sodium, distinct from sodium’s contribution to the electrochemical driving force. The mutation removes this regulatory response. In the tested mutant hearts and ventricular muscle cells, digoxin failed to produce the enhancement of contraction and calcium transients seen in wild-type preparations. This supports a role for exchanger regulation beyond a gradient-only account in those preparations. It does not establish a safer human therapy or a universal quantitative response. Calcium extrusion through NCX remains distinct from calcium uptake into SR through SERCA.

The epistemic lesson is precise. Equation (2.6) determines the energy available for a specified ion transfer under stated conditions. It does not determine how many transport proteins are active, how they are regulated, or the intracellular calcium transient during a heartbeat. A correct thermodynamic direction can coexist with a wrong rate prediction. In the epithelial running case the same warning applies: an energetically favorable sodium–glucose cycle can fail to sustain absorption if transporter delivery, activation or basolateral exit is impaired.

2.10 Homeostasis means regulated activity, not inactivity L6 02:06

Homeostasis is maintenance of an internal environment compatible with function through ongoing physiological processes. It does not require every variable to remain constant. In a steady state, opposing rates balance and a selected state variable stays constant. At thermodynamic equilibrium, sustained net driving forces are absent. A cell maintaining sodium differences through ATP use can be near a steady state while far from equilibrium. Its continued ATP consumption is part of the explanation for the stable concentrations, not a contradiction of them.

A feedback account needs a specified controlled variable, such as glucose concentration or arterial pressure; a sensor responsive to that variable or a related signal; an information-processing/controller function; and an effector that changes a process influencing the variable. A disturbance changes the variable independently of the corrective response. A reference is the value or condition against which a response is organized. The introductory homeostasis framework identifies sensor, controller and effector; real regulation can distribute these functions across cells rather than house them in one anatomical box.

Negative feedback means that an effect of a deviation returns through the loop to oppose that deviation. “Negative” describes the loop sign, not whether every arrow points downward. If glucose rises, a response increasing glucose uptake is a negative feedback contribution because increased removal opposes the original rise. A falling hormone concentration can equally belong to negative feedback if it reduces a process that had been driving the controlled variable downward.

The epithelial running case now becomes a control problem. A nutrient disturbance changes extracellular availability. Signals can alter carrier abundance, metabolism or transport gradients, changing the terms in (2.1). The question is whether those changes oppose or reinforce the disturbance, how quickly they act, and whether their capacity is sufficient. Naming a hormone is not enough: its target process and the sign of its effect on the controlled variable must be specified.

2.11 A minimal feedback model shows residual error L6 04:01

Construct an intentionally generic model, not a glucose, pressure or patient model. Let \(x(t)\) be deviation of a controlled quantity from a reference, measured in arbitrary units, au. Let \(d\) be a constant disturbance adding to the deviation at rate au/s. Let \(a\ge0\) be a passive removal coefficient, in s⁻¹. The controller measures \(x\) without delay or noise and commands an effector contribution \(u=-kx\), where \(k\ge0\), in s⁻¹, is feedback gain. Positive deviation therefore produces negative corrective action. Assume the effector can supply this action without saturation. The model is

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=d-ax+u=d-(a+k)x. \tag{2.12} \]

If \(a+k>0\), define \(x_\infty=d/(a+k)\), the steady deviation. Subtract this value from the state by setting \(y=x-x_\infty\). Because \(x_\infty\) is constant, (2.12) becomes \(dy/dt=-(a+k)y\). Integration gives

\[ x(t)=\frac{d}{a+k}+\left[x(0)-\frac{d}{a+k}\right]e^{-(a+k)t}, \quad \tau_{\rm fb}=\frac1{a+k}. \tag{2.13} \]

The exponent predicts stable decay of deviations from the new steady state. Feedback increases the restoring rate and reduces the steady deviation under a maintained disturbance. It does not generally eliminate that deviation. If proportional control returned \(x\) to exactly zero while \(d\ne0\), the controller contribution \(-kx\) would disappear even though the disturbance persisted. Perfect adaptation requires another mechanism, such as an appropriately implemented integral response, or a changed disturbance balance.

Choose synthetic \(d=1\) au/s and \(a=0.05\) s⁻¹, starting at \(x(0)=0\). With no feedback, \(k=0\), the steady deviation is 20 au and the time constant 20 s. With \(k=0.15\) s⁻¹, they become 5 au and 5 s. At 10 s, the deviations are respectively 7.869387 and 4.323324 au. The controlled trajectory rises toward 5 au under the sustained disturbance; it does not return to zero. Labeling a rising trace “failed negative feedback” would therefore be an error: comparison with the unregulated response reveals what the feedback accomplished.

The model also exposes a measurement ambiguity. A stable trace alone cannot identify feedback gain. Only the sum \(a+k\) appears in this one-state trajectory. A stronger passive removal process with weak feedback can produce the same curve as weaker passive removal with strong feedback. Separating the mechanisms requires an independent estimate of passive removal or a selective intervention on the feedback route. This is a practical example of identifiability, whether different parameter combinations can be distinguished by the available observations.

More feedback gain is not universally safer or more stable. Equation (2.12) assumes instantaneous sensing and action, a single state, linear effects and unlimited actuator capacity. Delays and additional dynamic states can produce overshoot or oscillation even when the loop sign is negative. A real physiological controller can also change its reference with context. Those possibilities do not invalidate the simple derivation; they specify what the derivation has excluded.

Static feedback sequence: a positive disturbance raises the measured deviation; the sensor reports that rise; the controller commands negative effector action; removal increases; the rise slows; a nonzero steady error remains under a continued disturbance. In the animation, the numerical trace and displayed effector command are computed from the same \(x(t)\), so the diagram cannot silently show recovery while its curve shows continued divergence.

2.12 Positive feedback needs a stopping condition L6 06:05

Positive feedback reinforces a process through a returning effect. In the childbirth example, cervical stretch can promote neural signals that increase oxytocin release; oxytocin promotes uterine contraction, which can increase cervical stretch. Oxytocin is synthesized in hypothalamic neurons and released into blood from their posterior-pituitary terminals. The sampled L6 slide already classifies this as positive feedback; it should not be “corrected” into negative feedback. The stopping event in the simplified birth loop is delivery removing the sustained stretch input. The neurobiological account of oxytocin and childbirth supports this bounded anatomical sequence; the account includes evidence from multiple species, so it is not adopted as a universal quantitative human labor model.

For a sign comparison only, replace the corrective term in (2.12) by \(+kx\). The linear equation becomes \(dx/dt=d+(k-a)x\). If \(k>a\), a positive deviation grows rather than decays when \(d=0\). This does not predict an infinitely contracting uterus: a linear equation lacks anatomy, finite resources, saturation, changing inputs and termination. Positive feedback can be useful for completing an event precisely because it amplifies rather than holds a variable near a reference.

Coagulation provides another lecture example: activated components can recruit or activate further components locally. Its useful action is bounded by spatial organization and regulatory processes, not a mandate that clotting spread indefinitely. The next blood chapters examine those mechanisms. Treating the sign label as a moral judgment—negative good, positive bad—would miss why a body needs both disturbance resistance and event completion.

Likewise, a variable changing in a beneficial direction is not sufficient evidence of positive feedback. The heart rate rising during exercise can support increased oxygen delivery, but the observation does not identify the sign of every contributing loop. Feedforward commands, meaning anticipatory inputs that do not wait for the controlled error, can act alongside feedback. A plotted rise and fall after activity contains combined mechanisms; it is not a direct measurement of a single controller coefficient.

2.13 Pressure and glucose require tissue-specific effectors L6 10:13

The L6 pressure diagram identifies pressure disturbance, receptor signaling, neural processing, and heart/vessel effectors. In a negative-feedback account of a pressure rise, the return pathway should tend to reduce pressure. A reduction in heart rate can contribute, but heart rate is not pressure itself. Blood volume, stroke volume, vascular resistance and their regulation also matter. The source schematic supplies an architectural example, not calibrated gains or a guarantee that every pressure disturbance evokes one identical trace.

Glucose regulation links extracellular conditions back to cellular transport. Pancreatic beta cells produce insulin; alpha cells produce glucagon. Insulin supports glucose handling in tissue-specific ways, including GLUT4-dependent uptake in muscle and fat. Glucagon promotes hepatic glucose output; hepatic means pertaining to the liver. Glycogen breakdown can release stored glucose equivalents, while gluconeogenesis makes glucose from noncarbohydrate precursors. These are different reaction terms in the balance. Endotext, glucagon physiology, provides the liver-centered mechanism; the accessible indexed extract specifically notes the importance of glycogen availability.

After a nutrient input, increasing removal and storage while reducing endogenous production can oppose a glucose rise. During fasting, increasing appropriate endogenous supply can oppose a fall. Neither branch is an instruction for all tissues to do the same thing. A hormone concentration is a signal; glucose is a transported and metabolized substrate. Saying that insulin “carries glucose” merges two different roles. In (2.1), a signal changes the functions defining a rate; it is not necessarily a molecule counted as part of that glucose flow.

Diabetes cannot be reduced to absence of one membrane doorway. The NIDDK overview distinguishes inadequate insulin production from impaired insulin use and identifies multiple disease types. This introductory distinction was checked on 2026-11-04. It does not establish diagnostic thresholds, treatment targets or an individualized explanation of a glucose trace. The generic arbitrary-unit feedback example remains deliberately separate from those clinical quantities.

For our absorptive cell, whole-body control ultimately changes the boundary conditions and rate laws: substrate supply, hormone signals, transport-protein availability and metabolic demand. A cell can therefore have a locally functional transporter while being embedded in an inadequately regulated organism. Conversely, a circulating signal cannot repair every damaged protein-processing pathway. The final section locates those failures without turning a list of organelles into a list of single-cause diseases.

2.14 Organelle dysfunction is a causal question L6 18:14

An organelle dysfunction is impaired performance of a subcellular structure. To explain disease causally, identify the altered molecular process, the compartment in which its effect first occurs, the resulting cellular failure, and the evidence connecting that failure to tissue dysfunction. “Mitochondria are involved” does not distinguish a primary inherited defect from secondary stress in an already diseased cell. It also does not establish that every person with a broad disease label shares one mechanism.

Mitochondria contribute to energy conversion and other cellular processes. A defect reducing ATP supply can compromise ATP-consuming transport, alter ion gradients and stress additional compartments. This is a plausible chain in the running cell, but its rate and dominance require measurements. The L6 list spanning Alzheimer disease, muscular dystrophy, ALS, diabetes and cancer cannot establish that all are uniform primary mitochondrial diseases. Association, contributing mechanism and sufficient cause are different evidentiary claims. The introductory model supplies no basis for reducing those diverse conditions to one organelle or one treatment.

A lysosome is an acidic degradative compartment with enzymes that process selected substrates. In a lysosomal storage disease, a defined defect can impair degradation or related handling, allowing material to accumulate. For Gaucher disease, the NLM gene–mechanism account links GBA1 variants, reduced glucocerebrosidase activity and accumulation of glucocerebroside. Different forms affect different tissues. This is more informative than calling lysosomes “suicide bags”: normal controlled degradation is not equivalent to indiscriminate destruction of the cell.

The lecture additionally names Fabry, Niemann–Pick, Pompe and Tay–Sachs diseases. These names indicate a family of degradation/handling problems, not interchangeable enzyme deficiencies or identical clinical courses. They are retained as navigation topics without inventing an unsourced enzyme table. In a minimal substrate balance, impaired removal raises stored material when production continues. That mathematical consequence supports the accumulation logic; it does not identify the deficient enzyme in an untested person.

Ribosomopathies involve defects in ribosome production or function and related pathways. The source's Diamond–Blackfan anemia example illustrates why a broadly used molecular machine can have tissue-selective consequences. The NLM account of Diamond–Blackfan anemia connects ribosomal-protein gene changes to impaired red-cell production. It should not be reduced to hemoglobin molecules simply failing to pass through a membrane. Marrow production, protein synthesis, cellular stress and developmental context occur at different explanatory levels.

ER processing can also affect a protein whose ultimate job is at the plasma membrane. CFTR is a membrane anion-channel protein; variants causing cystic fibrosis can alter its quantity or function, and some variants impair folding and delivery. The NLM cystic-fibrosis account supports the gene/channel relationship; the indexed abstract of Cheng et al.'s 1990 original study reports defective processing of selected mutant proteins in transfected cells. The important compartmental lesson is that “ER defect” and “membrane-protein defect” need not denote mutually exclusive diseases. For a particular variant, the causal step could be processing, trafficking or channel function; one must establish which.

Two source examples need an etiological correction. HIV infection has a viral cause, and shingles is reactivation of varicella-zoster virus. They are not adopted. Establishing a prevalence change requires consistent diagnostic criteria and a denominator; establishing cause requires more than parallel changes in exposure and case recognition. Mechanistic education is strongest when it makes those missing steps visible.

2.16 Problems with worked solutions

All numerical problems use the chapter's synthetic systems. None is a dosing calculation or a patient assessment.

Problem 2.1. Find the missing amount

Two fixed-volume compartments have \(V_o=2\) L and \(V_i=1\) L. Initially they contain 10 and 0 mmol/L of a nonreacting solute. A report claims both eventually reach 5 mmol/L in a closed system. Diagnose the error, calculate the correct equilibrium, and explain why continuing molecular crossings do not contradict equilibrium.

Worked solution

Initially outside contains \(2\times10=20\) mmol and inside zero. The proposed final state contains \((2+1)\times5=15\) mmol, losing 5 mmol without a route or reaction. In a closed passive system, equal final concentration \(c_*\) must satisfy \(3c_*=20\), so \(c_*=20/3=6.666667\) mmol/L. The error is an unweighted mean of concentrations. Molecules still cross each way through a permeable route, but the mean opposing transfer rates are equal, giving zero net transfer. The result assumes fixed volumes, no binding or reaction, and a solute that can cross the membrane.

Problem 2.2. Derive a half-time and test a geometric change

Using the same volumes and \(g=0.1\) L/s, derive the time for the concentration difference to halve. Predict what happens if exchange area doubles while thickness, material properties and volumes remain fixed. Does the equilibrium concentration change?

Worked solution

The difference obeys \(\Delta(t)=\Delta(0)e^{-t/\tau}\), with \(\tau^{-1}=0.1(1/2+1)=0.15\) s⁻¹. Setting \(\Delta(t)/\Delta(0)=1/2\) gives \(t_{1/2}=\ln2/0.15=4.620981\) s. Since \(g=Ap\), doubling area doubles the decay rate and halves the half-time. The equilibrium remains \(20/3\) mmol/L because neither total amount nor volumes changed. Faster equilibration and a changed equilibrium are separate predictions. The area result relies on fixed permeability and independent exchange area; it need not hold if doubling area also changes mixing or transporter density.

Problem 2.3. Separate gating from thermodynamics

At 310 K, potassium concentrations inside/outside are 140/4 mmol/L and voltage is −70 mV. A potassium-selective channel opens. Determine the favorable net transfer direction. Find the voltage at which this ion has zero electrochemical driving force. Explain what cannot be inferred from either result.

Worked solution

Using (2.6), \(RT\ln(140/4)+\mathcal F(-0.070)=+2.409877\) kJ/mol for inward potassium. Outward transfer has the opposite, negative energy, so net outward movement is favored if a conducting pathway is available. Setting this energy to zero gives \(E_K=(RT/\mathcal F)\ln(4/140)=-94.976619\) mV. The more-negative voltage supplies enough electrical attraction inward to balance the chemical drive outward. These results do not specify channel number, open probability, current magnitude, full resting membrane potential or the time course of voltage after opening. Holding voltage fixed is an imposed condition of the energy comparison.

Problem 2.4. Locate the energy source and the boundary

A cell imports one glucose together with one sodium. At 310 K and −60 mV, sodium concentrations are 15/145 mmol/L and glucose is tenfold higher inside. Is the selected tightly coupled inward cycle energetically favorable? Explain how a functioning Na/K pump supports the process. Then contrast NCX calcium extrusion with SERCA uptake.

Worked solution

One inward sodium contributes −11.636614 kJ per mole of coupled cycles. Neutral glucose moving tenfold uphill contributes \(RT\ln 10=5.934875\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}\). Adding the two terms gives \(\Delta G_{\mathrm{cycle}}=-11.636614+5.934875=-5.701739\ \mathrm{kJ/mol}\); the selected tightly coupled inward cycle is therefore energetically favorable. Sodium entry consumes the gradient. The Na/K pump spends ATP free energy to restore sodium extrusion, making uptake indirectly dependent on maintained chemical energy. Favorable energy does not determine the transport rate or guarantee sustained absorption without glucose exit and replenished boundaries. NCX in its forward extrusion mode exchanges extracellular sodium for cytosolic calcium across the plasma membrane, removing calcium from the cell. SERCA uses ATP across the SR membrane, moving calcium from cytosol into the SR lumen while retaining it inside the cell. Both can lower cytosolic calcium, but only NCX extrusion removes calcium from the whole cell.

Problem 2.5. Infer what a recovery curve cannot identify

A constant disturbance of 1 au/s acts on the feedback model, starting at zero deviation. A fitted trajectory has time constant 5 s and tends to 5 au. Can the curve uniquely establish passive removal \(a=0.05\) s⁻¹ and gain \(k=0.15\) s⁻¹? Why is the nonzero steady error compatible with negative feedback? Propose a discriminating experiment.

Worked solution

The trajectory identifies \(a+k=1/5=0.2\) s⁻¹. Its steady value also gives \(d/(a+k)=1/0.2=5\) au, confirming the same combined rate rather than a second independent constraint. Many nonnegative pairs with sum 0.2 give that curve, so the individual parameters are not identifiable from it. Negative feedback reduces error; proportional control alone need not eliminate an error under a continuing disturbance. A selective reversible intervention that disables the feedback pathway while leaving passive removal unchanged would expose \(a\) from the new time constant, after which subtraction identifies \(k\). The intervention assumption must itself be tested: if disabling the controller also changes passive metabolism, that inference fails.

Problem 2.6. Repair a causal explanation

A proposed diagram says: “digoxin lowers intracellular sodium, NCX moves calcium into SR, and positive feedback brings blood glucose back to its set point. Any organelle-associated disease is caused by pollution.” Locate each unsupported or incorrect step. Give the minimal evidence needed to improve the final assertion.

Worked solution

Na/K ATPase inhibition reduces sodium extrusion, tending to raise intracellular sodium. In the selected forward NCX mode, calcium goes across plasma membrane to extracellular fluid, not directly into SR. SERCA performs ATP-dependent SR uptake. A loop that opposes a glucose deviation is negative feedback; positive feedback reinforces a process and needs an endpoint or limiting mechanism. Finally, organelle association does not establish one environmental cause. A stronger etiological account would name a disease, define exposure and outcome, compare appropriate populations with confounding addressed, and connect molecular evidence to the phenotype. A defined inherited enzyme defect, for example, can support a causal chain without pollution being its established cause. The source's slide/caption disagreement over heart failure versus cardiac arrest also requires evidence-specific attribution rather than assuming all source modalities said the same thing.
